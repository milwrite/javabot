Verification Gates for Trustworthy Scholarly AI: Provenance, References, Completeness, and Durable Syndication

AI systems that generate or help draft scholarly texts now operate within an environment where citation accuracy, provenance tracking, and long‑term accessibility are central to research integrity and infrastructure.5,8Designing these systems to pass through explicit “verification gates” is essential to prevent hallucinated citations, incomplete or biased reference lists, truncated outputs, and fragile dissemination that cannot be reliably found or trusted over time.5,8Existing infrastructures around persistent identifiers such as DOIs, reference linking frameworks like Crossref, provenance models such as W3C PROV, and emerging tools for AI citation checking provide a concrete foundation from which to define such gates in an operationally precise way.4,10,14This report synthesizes those infrastructures and recent work on hallucination detection to propose a layered architecture of verification gates that a scholarly AI system should implement before its outputs are treated as citable scholarly artifacts.5,9,11## 1. Context: Why Verification Gates Are Now Essential

The rise of large language models in scientific communication has exposed a structural mismatch between probabilistic text generation and the strict evidentiary standards of scholarship.5,8LLMs routinely produce plausible‑looking bibliographic records that do not correspond to real publications or that contain subtly corrupted metadata, a phenomenon termed citation hallucination or LLM‑induced hallucinated citations.5,8Studies of models such as ChatGPT and Bard have found that around 30–40% of their generated citations are wrong or completely fabricated when used naïvely, underscoring that surface fluency is not a reliable signal of bibliographic validity.8At the same time, editorial bodies such as the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) emphasize that authors are responsible for ensuring their references are accurate, traceable, and that cited works genuinely support associated claims, even when AI tools were involved in drafting.11,12Verification gates are structured checkpoints where AI outputs are systematically compared against authoritative external resources, such as Crossref, PubMed, OpenAlex, DataCite, or institutional repositories, rather than being accepted at face value.1,2,3Tools like Citely and similar AI citation checkers operationalize this idea by parsing outputs, extracting citations, and verifying them against large scholarly databases, marking each reference as verified, partially matched, or fabricated based on metadata agreement.1,2,3Recent research systems such as CiteCheck go further by grounding each citation in retrieved scholarly records and using a secondary LLM to classify it into exact, minor, or major hallucination categories, showing that structured verification can dramatically improve reliability.9In parallel, work in scholarly LLMs and evaluations has highlighted that mitigation strategies such as retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG), post‑generation verification, and neurosymbolic fact‑checking can reduce the rate and impact of hallucinations when systematically integrated.5,8The need for verification extends beyond citation correctness to the overall structural integrity and persistence of AI‑generated scholarly documents.4,10Outputs must not be truncated mid‑section; they must present complete reference lists rather than truncated or biased subsets; and, once accepted, they should be made durably findable and citable through persistent identifiers and archival infrastructures.4,6,16Persistent identifier ecosystems such as DOIs, as defined and managed by organizations like DataCite and Crossref, are designed precisely to make scholarly content FAIR—findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable—over long time horizons, and they impose strong requirements on metadata completeness and persistence.4,10,13Finally, provenance models such as the W3C PROV Data Model and nanopublication architectures provide rigorously defined ways of describing how a piece of data or text was produced, which agents were involved, and how it relates to prior knowledge, all of which are increasingly critical for judging the trustworthiness of AI‑assisted research artifacts.14,18,19## 2. Conceptual Foundations: Provenance, Identifiers, and Reference Completeness

A first pillar for defining verification gates is the notion of provenance as a formal, interoperable description of how a scholarly artifact was produced.14The W3C PROV Data Model defines provenance as information describing entities, activities, and people involved in producing a piece of data or thing, supporting assessments about its quality, reliability, or trustworthiness.14PROV‑DM introduces core structures for expressing entities, activities, agents, derivations, and relationships among them, and it is designed so that different domain‑specific provenance formats can be translated into a common core model and exchanged between systems.14This means an AI research system that records which retrievers, prompts, and human reviewers contributed to a generated document can represent that process in PROV terms, making it machine‑interpretable and portable across repositories and platforms.14Nanopublications extend this provenance‑centric view by defining a minimal, atomic unit of scientific knowledge that bundles a formal assertion with its provenance and publication metadata as named RDF graphs.18,19In the nanopublication model, each nanopublication consists of three core named graphs: an assertion graph that expresses a single claim or data relationship as RDF triples, a provenance graph that describes how that assertion was obtained (methods, data sources, responsible agents), and a publication‑info graph that provides metadata about the nanopublication itself, such as creator, creation time, and license.18,19This granularity enables citations and credit at the level of individual assertions rather than whole articles, and it ensures that every claim is tightly linked to explicit provenance information.18,19Tools and libraries for nanopublications allow developers to specify provenance attributes such as “wasDerivedFrom” relationships or attribution to an ORCID‑identified author via PROV terms, showing how provenance metadata can be programmatically attached to generated assertions.15,18For AI‑generated scholarly text, adopting either full nanopublication structures or PROV‑inspired provenance graphs around sections, claims, or citations can form a powerful verification gate, because any output that lacks its expected provenance relations can be programmatically flagged for review.14,15,18A second pillar is the infrastructure of persistent identifiers, particularly DOIs, which are crucial for durable public syndication and for verifying that cited sources correspond to real objects.4,10,13DataCite’s DOI registration policies underscore that DOIs should be assigned only to content for which an organization is responsible, that DOIs must be unique and not duplicated across already‑DOI‑assigned objects, and that each DOI must resolve to a publicly available landing page with at least minimal metadata including creator(s), title, publisher, publication year, and resource type.4DOIs can be assigned not only to traditional articles but also to datasets, software, audiovisual materials, images, and other research outputs, enabling a broad range of scholarly content to be persistently identified and cited.4,13OpenAIRE’s guidelines for resource identifiers similarly emphasize that resource identifiers should use stable schemes like DOI, URI, URL, or Handle, and that the identifier should resolve to a stable landing or jump‑off page, with other related identifiers captured separately as alternative or related identifiers.13Reference completeness, in turn, is governed by expectations from both infrastructure providers and editorial bodies.6,7,11Crossref’s guidance on reference metadata completeness encourages publishers to deposit all references, not only those that currently have DOIs, because references are valuable in their own right and may later become linkable as archival content receives DOIs.6Crossref members are required to create outbound DOI links in references when DOIs are available, and they provide tools such as the Simple Text Query (STQ) interface and XML APIs to match reference strings against Crossref’s DOI registry.7,10Editorial guidance such as the ICMJE recommendations stresses that authors should provide direct references to original research sources whenever possible, avoid citing predatory or pseudo‑journals, verify references using bibliographic sources like PubMed or original publications, and ensure that references do not include retracted articles unless specifically noting the retraction.11Together, these frameworks suggest that a scholarly AI system must not only verify that each citation corresponds to a real, correctly described source, but should also make a good‑faith effort to produce a complete and non‑selective reference list in line with community standards.6,7,11## 3. Risk Landscape: Hallucinated Citations, Truncation, and Fragile Dissemination

Scholarly LLMs encounter a characteristic class of failure modes that are particularly damaging in academic contexts, most prominently hallucinated citations.5,8LLM‑induced hallucinated citations encompass both fully fabricated references that do not correspond to any real scholarly work and partially corrupted references where some metadata fields such as author, year, or DOI are incorrect or mismatched relative to a real publication.5,9The EmergentMind overview describes several subtypes, including total fabrication, partial attribute corruption, identifier hijacking where a DOI is assigned to the wrong paper, semantic hallucinations where the cited work’s content is mischaracterized, and placeholder hallucinations where generic labels are left in place of real citations.5These behaviors result from the model’s tendency to satisfy prompts by generating statistically plausible sequences rather than querying an authoritative bibliographic database, and they are exacerbated when prompts explicitly demand long reference lists without providing grounding.5,8Empirical studies reviewed in the “Hallucinations in Scholarly LLMs” paper report that a large fraction of AI‑generated citations are either incorrect or nonexistent unless rigorous mitigation is applied, confirming that hallucinations represent a systemic risk.8Beyond hallucinations, AI outputs are often structurally incomplete or truncated, especially in long‑form tasks like literature reviews or grant drafts.8Because many models operate under context length or token limits, long answers can be cut off mid‑sentence or mid‑section, leaving conclusions, methods, or reference lists incomplete.8Truncation is especially problematic when users copy such text into manuscripts, because missing sections or incomplete references may not be obvious without careful reading, and automated checks built only around citation validity will not detect structural gaps.8,9Additionally, LLMs may omit important prior work even when they do not fabricate citations, because their training data and decoding behavior can introduce visibility biases toward certain venues, languages, or time periods, undermining the completeness and fairness of reference lists.8,11A further dimension of risk concerns the long‑term discoverability and citable status of AI‑assisted outputs.4,16If a scholarly AI system generates a report that is then shared as a preprint, dataset descriptor, or institutional white paper, it must still be integrated into the broader identifier and preservation ecosystem to be reliably cited and preserved.4,13DataCite emphasizes that DOIs should resolve to public landing pages and that the underlying objects should be part of digital preservation programs with multiple redundant copies, ideally with checks for bit integrity over time.4Portico’s digital preservation service exemplifies this approach by ingesting content directly from publishers, normalizing it to preservation‑ready formats, and providing libraries with access to archived content when it becomes lost, orphaned, or abandoned, functioning as a kind of insurance policy for e‑resources.16Without similar attention to persistent identifiers and archival arrangements, AI‑generated scholarly outputs may circulate informally, be cited in ways that cannot be resolved, and eventually disappear, breaking the scholarly record.4,16## 4. Verification Gates for Source Provenance

The first class of concrete verification gates should focus on ensuring that every cited source has demonstrable provenance and that the AI system’s own generative process is transparently recorded.1,3,14At the pre‑generation stage, retrieval‑augmented generation can act as a soft verification gate by constraining the model to draw content and citations from a set of documents retrieved from trusted scholarly sources such as Crossref‑indexed journals, PubMed, Scopus, or other curated repositories.8In RAG, the model first identifies relevant documents using a retrieval system and then conditions generation explicitly on these documents, thereby grounding its statements and references in verifiable publications rather than relying solely on internal parameters.8Extensions such as RAG‑HAT incorporate hallucination‑aware tuning that penalizes unsupported claims during training, further reducing the likelihood of uncited or mis‑cited assertions, which makes downstream verification easier.8During generation, provenance‑aware prompting and structured output formats can form a second gate by requiring the model to produce, alongside each citation, an internal identifier linking the cited claim to one of the retrieved documents.8,9For example, the system can require that every in‑text citation refer to a document ID from the retrieval stage, which is then mapped to external identifiers such as DOIs or PubMed IDs through a resolution layer, ensuring that no citation appears without an underlying retrieved source.8,9Structured output formats such as JSON can include fields for “retrieved_source_ids” for each paragraph or sentence, enabling later transformation into PROV or nanopublication provenance graphs.14,18In effect, the generation step becomes a provenance‑aware activity in the PROV sense, where each textual entity (sentence, paragraph) is explicitly linked to the entities (retrieved papers) it was derived from.14Post‑generation, dedicated citation verification tools provide the most explicit gate for source provenance.1,2,3,9Services like Citely’s AI Citation Checker treat each reference as a claim to be verified, attempting DOI resolution against Crossref’s registry, matching titles and authors across PubMed and OpenAlex, and validating publication years and journal names against authoritative metadata; a citation is marked as verified only when an independent source confirms it.1,3Similarly, ProofreaderPro’s citation checker resolves DOIs via Crossref and matches titles, authors, and years against Semantic Scholar, returning a verdict and corrected citation for each entry.2Research frameworks such as CiteCheck generalize these ideas by retrieving candidate publications from multiple scholarly APIs and the web, then using a structured LLM verifier to compare the citation metadata to each candidate and assign one of three labels: exact match, minor hallucination (e.g., small metadata discrepancies), or major hallucination (no plausible match).9The combination of retrieval, structured comparison, and calibrated decision rules in CiteCheck demonstrates that automated verification can reliably distinguish true citations from hallucinations at scale, making this a natural verification gate before any AI‑generated manuscript is accepted into a repository or submission system.9Beyond verifying that cited sources exist and are correctly described, a rigorous scholarly AI system should implement provenance documentation for its own outputs using standards like PROV and nanopublications.14,18,19For each generated document or section, the system can create a provenance record specifying the activities (model inference, post‑editing), agents (models, human reviewers identified via ORCID), inputs (retrieved articles identified via DOIs), and derivation relationships between them, represented in PROV‑DM or PROV‑O.14,18Nanopublication frameworks show how assertion‑level provenance can be expressed as named RDF graphs, and existing libraries exemplify how to attribute assertions to specific ORCID‑identified agents or indicate derivation from prior nanopublications using PROV properties like “prov:wasDerivedFrom”.15,18,19Treating each substantial claim or consolidated finding in an AI‑generated report as a nanopublication, or at least as an entity annotated with similar provenance metadata, enables downstream systems and readers to inspect how the claim was constructed and which sources it rests on, forming a powerful provenance verification gate.14,18,19## 5. Verification Gates for Reference List Completeness

Ensuring that an AI‑generated scholarly output presents a complete and fair reference list requires both infrastructure‑level checks and alignment with editorial standards.6,7,11From an infrastructure perspective, Crossref recommends that publishers include all references, not just those with DOIs, when depositing metadata, and notes that references are valuable even if they cannot yet be matched to existing DOIs because archival content may receive DOIs later.6This implies that an AI system should not restrict its references to only DOI‑bearing works; it should be capable of citing books, older articles, datasets, and grey literature that may be identified by other persistent identifiers or even by well‑structured bibliographic descriptions without DOIs.4,6,13At the same time, Crossref’s reference linking policies require members to create outbound DOI links for references whenever possible, and they provide tools such as the Simple Text Query form and APIs to query DOIs for reference strings.7,10A reference completeness gate can therefore involve running all candidate references through Crossref and related services, ensuring that every citable work with a DOI is linked, while still retaining references that lack DOIs but pass other verification checks.6,7Editorial guidelines provide a complementary lens on completeness and quality of reference lists.11The ICMJE recommendations emphasize that authors should provide direct references to original research sources whenever possible and should avoid relying on abstracts or secondary summaries when primary research is available.11They also stress that references should not be used to promote self‑interest, that articles from predatory or pseudo‑journals should be avoided, and that AI‑generated material should not be cited as a primary source, even if AI tools were used in drafting.11,12Moreover, the ICMJE requires that authors verify references using bibliographic databases such as PubMed or original sources, and that they check not to cite retracted articles except when explicitly discussing the retraction.11A reference completeness gate in an AI system can encode these principles by checking whether key claims are supported by primary literature, scanning citations against retraction databases, and flagging references that appear in known predatory outlets or that are themselves AI‑generated content.8,11To move beyond binary notions of presence or absence, a sophisticated completeness gate might also consider coverage relative to the retrieved evidence and the topical scope requested by the user.8,9For instance, if the retrieval stage identified a set of highly cited or methodologically central papers on a topic, but the generated reference list omits several of them while including more marginal works, the system can flag this as potential incompleteness or bias.8While determining an objectively “complete” reference list is inherently subjective, quantitative heuristics such as ensuring that a high proportion of cited works correspond to the top‑k retrieved documents by relevance or citation count can provide a practical operationalization.8,9Additionally, reference lists can be checked for structural completeness, ensuring that they include all works cited in the main text, that there are no orphaned references, and that they conform to the numbering or author‑date schemes required by target venues, in line with standards described by bodies like ICMJE and NISO.11,17Finally, existing citation checker services already implement elements of completeness and correction that can be extended into verification gates.1,2,3For example, ProofreaderPro’s citation checker not only verifies entries but also outputs corrected citations in multiple styles, effectively enforcing stylistic completeness and consistency alongside factual accuracy.2Citely emphasizes that its academic source verification tool can trace claims, paragraphs, and research ideas back to discoverable scholarly sources, supporting a form of completeness where assertions are connected to as many relevant sources as feasible, not just a minimal single citation.3Integrating such tools into an AI system’s pipeline, with thresholds requiring that a high proportion of citations be verified and that key assertions have multiple supporting references, can operationalize a robust reference completeness gate.1,2,3## 6. Verification Gates Against Truncation and Structural Incompleteness

Non‑truncated, structurally coherent outputs are a prerequisite for reliable scholarly communication, yet AI systems are prone to truncation due to context limits and generation constraints.8A basic gate against truncation involves enforcing structural templates for outputs, such as requiring that a scientific report include sections like Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, and References, and then checking post‑generation that each section is present and ends with complete sentences rather than abrupt cut‑offs.11Since ICMJE and similar guidelines recommend that manuscripts present results in logical sequence with clear sections, tables, and figures, an AI system can use those expectations as a schema against which to validate generated content.11If any expected section is missing or ends in an incomplete sentence or paragraph, the system can either re‑invoke generation for the missing segment or flag the document as not passing the truncation gate.8More sophisticated truncation gates can incorporate semantic and format‑level checks.8For example, reference lists can be validated to ensure that they complete the numbering or alphabetical sequence implied by the in‑text citations; truncated reference sections often end mid‑entry or omit the final portion of the list, which can be detected by cross‑checking the highest citation number mentioned in the text against the number of entries in the references.8,11Similarly, headings and subheadings can be validated to ensure that they are followed by content, and that the final section, often the conclusion, ends with a complete sentence and paragraph rather than an ellipsis or abrupt stop.11These checks do not require external databases; they function as internal structural verification gates that ensure the document is syntactically and organizationally complete before more expensive external verification is applied.8Some truncation issues arise when AI systems are embedded into workflows that involve manual copying or API limits, rather than model limits per se.8For example, a user might copy only the first part of a generated report into a manuscript, or an upstream system might silently truncate the AI’s output due to field length restraints, leading to incomplete records in repositories or submission systems.8To guard against this, durable syndication infrastructures like DOIs and repository metadata frameworks typically require that deposited items include a complete landing page with minimally required fields, as emphasized by DataCite and OpenAIRE guidelines.4,13An AI‑integrated pipeline can leverage these requirements as a truncation gate by refusing to assign a DOI or finalize a repository deposit unless mandatory fields and expected structural elements are present and validated.4,13In this way, identifier assignment becomes contingent on passing a structural completeness gate, aligning model behavior with preservation and discoverability standards.4,13,16## 7. Verification Gates for Durable Public Syndication

Durable public syndication of AI‑generated scholarly content hinges on aligning with existing identifier and preservation infrastructures.4,13,16DataCite’s best practices stress that DOIs must resolve to publicly available landing pages that contain at least the core metadata elements—creators, title, publisher, publication year, and resource type—and that metadata should be updated if it changes over time.4They also recommend that DOIs be assigned only to unique content for which the registering organization has responsibility, and that relationships among different versions or related objects be expressed via related identifiers in the DOI metadata.4OpenAIRE similarly treats resource identifiers as mandatory elements in repository metadata, recommending that identifiers conform to formal schemes like DOI, Handle, URL, or URN and link to a stable landing or jump‑off page.13A durable syndication gate for AI outputs can therefore require that any document intended for long‑term citation be associated with a DOI or equivalent persistent identifier and a properly structured landing page, with metadata that captures relationships to underlying datasets, code, and prior versions.4,13Preservation services like Portico illustrate the infrastructural requirements behind durable access.16Portico maintains archiving agreements with publishers, ingests content directly, normalizes publisher XML to standardized tag sets such as the National Library of Medicine’s Journal Archiving Tag Set, and creates preservation metadata records to assure long‑term usability and discoverability.16It provides libraries with access to archived content when it becomes lost, orphaned, or abandoned, acting as an insurance policy for e‑resources and supporting post‑cancellation access arrangements.16For AI‑generated or AI‑assisted scholarly content to enjoy similar durability, depositing institutions or publishers must integrate such content into their preservation programs, which implies that the AI system’s outputs must be structurally and semantically clean enough to be ingested and normalized by archival infrastructures.4,16A preservation gate can thus verify that the generated document conforms to required formats and metadata standards before allowing deposition into long‑term archives.4,13,16Durable syndication also involves correct presentation and identification practices for e‑journals and other serials.17NISO’s recommended practice on the Presentation and Identification of E‑Journals (PIE‑J) emphasizes the accurate use of ISSNs, consistent title presentation, and robust citation practices to ensure that articles are correctly identified and linked over time.17AI systems integrated into journal workflows must therefore ensure that generated metadata and references correctly use ISSNs where appropriate, that journal titles align with official forms, and that citations conform to expected styles, facilitating downstream linking and indexing.10,17Crossref’s documentation on reference linking further notes that including DOIs as URLs in reference lists enables researchers to follow persistent links from references to current landing pages for cited works, and that best practice involves linking references for backfile content, preprints, books, and other resource types as well.10A syndication gate can thus involve validating that references include correctly formatted DOI URLs where available, in line with Crossref display guidelines, ensuring that the resulting document is well integrated into the DOI citation network.4,10Finally, provenance and attribution metadata are central to durable syndication in the era of AI.11,14,18ICMJE guidance requires that the use of AI for writing assistance be disclosed in acknowledgments and that AI not be listed as an author, with authors remaining responsible for all aspects of the work’s accuracy and integrity.11PROV‑DM and nanopublication models provide a natural way to encode such attributions, linking the generated document to the human authors, AI systems, and inputs that contributed to it.14,18,19Including ORCID identifiers for human authors and ROR identifiers for institutions in DOI metadata, as recommended by DataCite, further ties the document into the broader persistent identifier graph.4A final syndication gate can require that all AI‑related provenance, human authorship, affiliation, and funding information be included and validated before assigning identifiers and depositing the record, thereby ensuring that the document enters the scholarly ecosystem with transparent lineage and robust linkages.4,11,14## 8. Integrating Gates into an End‑to‑End Scholarly AI Pipeline

While each verification gate—provenance, reference completeness, truncation checks, and durable syndication—can be defined in isolation, their real power emerges when integrated into a coherent end‑to‑end pipeline.5,8,9Recent overviews of mitigation strategies for citation hallucinations recommend multi‑stage, multi‑source verification pipelines in which references are parsed, checked for existence and metadata consistency, validated against identifiers, and optionally subjected to semantic plausibility checks.5,8Systems such as CiteCheck demonstrate that a hybrid approach combining scholarly retrieval, structured LLM‑based comparison, and calibrated decision rules can achieve high recall in detecting synthetic hallucinations, suggesting that verification gates should be positioned both before and after generation rather than exclusively at the end.9Editorial integration and audit tools, including journal intake systems that validate all references and reviewer‑assistive dashboards to flag suspect citations, exemplify how these gates can be embedded into existing scholarly communication workflows.5,9In a mature scholarly AI pipeline, the first gate can involve RAG‑based retrieval from trusted databases, creating a candidate evidence set that both constrains generation and provides a basis for later completeness checks.8The second gate can operate during generation via structured prompting and output formats that force explicit mappings between textual claims and retrieved sources, setting up the data needed for subsequent provenance graphs and citation verification.8,14A third gate, immediately post‑generation, can run specialized citation checkers like Citely‑style or ProofreaderPro‑style services to validate each reference against Crossref, PubMed, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, and web search where necessary, flagging major and minor hallucinations and suggesting corrections.1,2,3,9A fourth gate can then examine reference completeness by checking coverage of key retrieved sources, presence of all cited works in the references, and alignment with editorial standards such as ICMJE’s requirements.6,7,11Structural completeness and truncation checks can form a fifth gate, ensuring that the document’s section structure is intact, that reference lists are not cut off, and that the text ends in complete sentences, drawing on schemas inspired by journal guidelines and repository metadata requirements.8,11,13A sixth gate can construct and validate provenance records using PROV‑DM or nanopublication structures, ensuring that all claims, citations, and sections have corresponding derivation and attribution metadata, including ORCID‑identified human authors and identifiers for input sources.14,18,19A final gate, preceding DOI assignment and archive deposition, can verify that landing page metadata is complete per DataCite and OpenAIRE guidelines, that references include resolved and correctly formatted DOIs, and that the content is suitable for ingestion into preservation systems like Portico.4,10,13,16Such a pipeline also supports monitoring and continuous improvement of AI systems via provenance‑based analytics.14By recording which gates fail and why—for example, frequent identifier hijacking or recurrent omission of key sources—developers can refine prompts, retrievers, and training data to reduce failure rates over time, aligning with the broader vision of retrieval‑grounded, provenance‑aware, and audit‑friendly scholarly LLM architectures.5,8,9Incorporating human‑in‑the‑loop review at critical gates, especially those involving semantic alignment and interpretive judgments, ensures that researchers and editors retain ultimate responsibility, consistent with ICMJE’s insistence that authors remain accountable for their work even when AI tools are used.11,12## 9. Conclusion

Scholarly AI research systems must evolve from generative engines that produce plausible text into provenance‑aware, infrastructure‑aligned components of the scholarly communication ecosystem.5,8Concrete verification gates provide the scaffolding for this evolution by turning high‑level principles—such as citation accuracy, reference completeness, structural integrity, and durable accessibility—into operational checkpoints enforced by code and policy.1,3,4Provenance gates grounded in PROV‑DM and nanopublication models ensure that every claim and citation is traceable back to identifiable inputs and responsible agents, while citation verification gates inspired by tools like Citely, ProofreaderPro, and CiteCheck systematically eliminate hallucinated or corrupted references by checking them against Crossref, PubMed, OpenAlex, and similar databases.1,2,3,9,14,18Reference completeness gates align AI outputs with Crossref and ICMJE expectations, encouraging full and fair coverage of the literature, including works without DOIs and primary sources, while truncation and structural gates ensure that outputs conform to the sectioned, logically ordered formats expected of scholarly articles and repository records.6,7,8,11Finally, durable syndication gates tie AI‑generated outputs into the persistent identifier and preservation infrastructures that underpin long‑term scholarly memory, leveraging DOI and resource identifier best practices, e‑journal identification standards, and services such as Portico to make AI‑assisted scholarship both citable and resilient.4,10,13,16,17When integrated into an end‑to‑end pipeline, these gates do more than catch errors: they create feedback loops through which AI systems can be evaluated, tuned, and governed according to the same norms that have long guided human scholarship.5,8,11By embracing these verification gates as first‑class design requirements, developers, publishers, and researchers can harness the productivity benefits of AI while maintaining, and in some respects enhancing, the integrity, transparency, and durability of the scholarly record.4,5,8,11,14
