Model-Agnostic AI Infrastructure at CUNY: A Comprehensive Review of the CUNY AI Lab GitHub Organization, Sandbox Ecosystem, and Key Projects

The CUNY AI Lab (CAIL) has emerged as a distinctive experiment in building university-scale, model-agnostic AI infrastructure that is grounded in open-weight models, privacy protections, and critical pedagogical design rather than commercial platform logics.3,13,18From its public-facing Sandbox and Model Registry to its GitHub-hosted projects such as Stem Splitter and the CAIL-Pi workshop integration package, the Lab constructs what can be understood as a “doorway platform” that lets instructors, students, and researchers move fluidly between chat interfaces, bespoke tools, and programmatic API access while retaining institutional control and transparency.3,11,14This report synthesizes the evolution of that infrastructure with a particular focus on the Lab’s GitHub organization, its model API and access practices, and selected projects and workshop series that extend the Sandbox into specialized pedagogical and research environments.1,2,4Throughout, attention is paid to the role of open-source development practices—diffs, pull requests, and modular repositories—in enabling iterative refinement and collaborative governance of AI tools inside the university, and to the broader promise of model-agnostic design for academic work across disciplines.3,12,15## The CUNY AI Lab: Mission, Institutional Context, and GitHub Presence

Institutional Mandate and Infrastructure Philosophy

The CUNY AI Lab is described as a faculty- and staff-led initiative at the CUNY Graduate Center that “builds and maintains AI infrastructure for teaching, research, and academic work across CUNY.”3,13This framing immediately situates the Lab not as a one-off service provider but as an infrastructural project, emphasizing durable systems, shared governance, and alignment with academic values.3,13The Lab’s public materials highlight three priorities in particular: privacy, transparency, and thoughtful integration of AI into academic workflows, which distinguish it from mainstream commercial AI platforms that often rely on data retention and opaque training pipelines.13,18These priorities are operationalized through technical decisions such as the exclusive reliance on open-weight models, meaning models whose parameters are publicly available and can be inspected and hosted by multiple providers, and a gateway configuration that prohibits training on user prompts and responses and enforces zero data retention.12,14,18The Lab is explicitly described as being in a pilot phase, with limited resources, and offers access to Graduate Center students, faculty, and staff along with selected faculty and staff from other CUNY campuses whose work aligns with the Lab’s research and pedagogical framework.5,18This access structure underscores the Lab’s dual role as both an experimental site for AI methods and a production infrastructure for teaching and research, since pilot status allows iterative testing of tools while the institutional framing requires clarity about eligibility, spending limits, and support.5,18The FAQ and request-access pages emphasize that all Lab tools use CUNY Login for identity management, but that individual tools receive opaque IDs rather than exposing usernames to backend services, thereby decoupling personal identity from tool-level logs.5,18Logs are described as recording events rather than content, and the Lab’s model gateway does not store prompts or responses, which is central to the Lab’s privacy commitments.18Alongside its hosted platforms, the Lab maintains a GitHub presence that aggregates source code for its Tools Portal applications, accessibility pipelines, and affiliated research code.3,4,11The “Links” page explicitly points to GitHub repositories for projects such as Agent Studio, Site Studio, and Manifold Companion, describing them as the source repositories for the Lab’s apps and platform.4This public GitHub organization both reflects and enables the Lab’s infrastructure philosophy: by publishing code under open-source licenses and exposing commit histories, the Lab invites inspection, reuse, and adaptation of its tools by the wider academic and technical community.3,4The GitHub presence is thus not merely a convenience but a core component of the Lab’s attempt to build transparent and auditable AI ecosystems inside a public university system.3,4,11### GitHub Organization as a Hub for Tools and Pedagogical Artifacts

The GitHub repositories referenced in the Lab’s materials represent a mix of production tools, workshop code, and pedagogical prototypes that collectively map the breadth of its activities.1,4,6,7For example, the Stem Splitter repository is introduced as a stem-separation web application created for music students as part of the T(h)inkering track of the 2026 Critical AI Literacy Institute, allowing users to upload songs and receive isolated stems in a synchronized browser-based mixer.1The Tools Portal’s Agent Studio and Site Studio applications are backed by GitHub repositories that implement, respectively, an AI research assistant for tasks involving data and academic databases, and an AI-assisted academic site builder for creating portfolios, CVs, and research pages through conversation.3,4,11The Lab’s GitHub also hosts open-source pipelines for media accessibility, such as the Manifold Companion project, a multi-user OCR platform for manuscript digitization built with Express and React and integrated with AWS Bedrock models.4In addition to application code, the Lab publishes workshop decks and teaching materials on GitHub, including the “Creative Coding with Generative AI” deck and the “Critical Play with LLMs” materials.6,7These repositories demonstrate how the Lab’s tooling is embedded into pedagogical sequences: the Creative Coding deck, for instance, walks participants through setting up accounts in the CAIL Sandbox, confirming access, and then using a GitHub project (cuny-ai-lab/creative-coding) as a basis for AI-assisted coding exercises.7Similarly, the Critical Play materials include an interactive Jeopardy-style emulator for probing the knowledge boundaries of language models, along with source code and workshop materials maintained in a GitHub repository.6By combining infrastructure code, application logic, and teaching artifacts within a single organizational namespace, the Lab’s GitHub organization serves as an integrated record of its experiments in AI-mediated pedagogy and research, and an archive of diffs and pull requests that document how those experiments evolve over time.1,4,6,7## Evolution of API Model Access and the Doorway Platform

Model Registry and the CAIL Model API

At the core of the Lab’s technical infrastructure is the CAIL Model API, a programmatic endpoint that exposes multiple open-weight models for use in custom tools, workflows, and external services.11,12,13The Model Registry provides a public catalog of current models available through the Lab, listing each model’s capabilities, context limits, and token prices, and providing the exact API ID required for programmatic access.12,11This registry is explicitly described as covering open-weight models that are served with zero data retention, meaning that prompts and outputs are not stored for training or long-term logging, and that each model can be audited or hosted by different providers as needed.12,18The ability to compare context limits and token costs across models allows instructors and developers to make informed decisions about which models to use for specific tasks, balancing performance and budget constraints.12,18The Model API documentation describes how users can create personal API keys, find current model IDs, and send chat requests, thereby making the Lab’s models accessible through OpenAI-compatible client libraries and custom scripts.11,14Approved users manage their keys via the Model Access interface, which is referenced in the FAQ and request-access materials, and are given a monthly spending limit—typically around five dollars—that caps their model usage and provides transparency around resource consumption.5,18The Model API’s `/v1/models` endpoint is highlighted in the Tools page, indicating that users can programmatically inspect available models and then invoke them through standard RESTful calls, enhancing discoverability and enabling dynamic tool configurations.14As an infrastructural design, this Model Registry and API combination embodies the Lab’s model-agnostic philosophy, because it decouples the concept of “chat with an AI” or “run an AI-assisted workflow” from any single provider or proprietary model.12,13Since the Lab uses open-weight models and exposes their IDs in a consistent API, developers can swap models, experiment with alternatives, or add new models as they become available without changing the high-level logic of their tools.12,14This is particularly important for academic workflows, where reproducibility and long-term availability matter more than following commercial product cycles, and where the ability to audit models’ behavior and training is central to critical AI literacy.3,12,18### Sandbox and Tools Portal as Doorways to Models

The CAIL Sandbox is presented as a chat platform for teaching and research that provides CUNY students, faculty, and staff with an AI chat interface and a curated set of open-weight models.13,14Built on Open WebUI, a community-driven chat interface for running AI models, the Sandbox offers a familiar conversational environment while embedding the Lab’s privacy and transparency commitments into its configuration.14The Tools page explains that while chats are privately stored in users’ Sandbox accounts, all requests to providers are configured for zero data retention and explicitly prohibit model training on those inputs.14,18This design allows the Sandbox to function as a “doorway” to the Model API: instructors who first test workflows in the chat environment can later export system prompts or conversational patterns into custom tools that call the same models through the API.11,14Alongside the Sandbox, the Tools Portal advertises Agent Studio, Site Studio, and Media Tools as specialized doorways for research and publishing work.3,13,14Agent Studio is described as an AI research assistant that supports tasks involving sources, data, files, drafts, and visualizations, integrating search over academic databases and exploration of data alongside model-based analysis.3,4Site Studio, by contrast, assists in building academic websites, portfolios, and CVs through conversation, reflecting the Lab’s interest in AI-mediated composition and design.4,13Media Tools provide transcription, image description, and document OCR, including high-accuracy optical character recognition and accessibility pipelines for PDFs and scanned images.4,14All of these tools are connected back to the same underlying model gateway and Model Registry, which means that they share the Lab’s open-weight, zero-retention model stack even as they present different user interfaces and workflows.3,11,14The Sandbox and Tools Portal thus collectively form what can be called a “doorway platform” for the Lab’s models: a set of entry points—chat, research assistant, site builder, media processing—that route requests through a common, auditable, and configurable model gateway.3,13,14This architecture allows instructors to choose between ad hoc conversational use, structured workflows in Agent Studio, or custom tool development via the Model API, while students can engage with AI in contexts carefully designed around their courses and disciplines.3,5,11In turn, the GitHub repositories for these tools encode the logic of how they interact with the model gateway, with diffs and pull requests allowing the Lab’s developers and collaborators to refine those interactions over time.4,15### Request Access, Eligibility, and Resource Governance

Access to the Sandbox, Model API, and Tools Portal is orchestrated through a single application process described on the request-access page.5,13Prospective users—primarily Graduate Center students, faculty, and staff—submit an application that covers the chat Sandbox, API keys for the Lab’s model endpoint, Agent Studio, Site Studio, and Media Tools, rather than applying separately for each tool.5,18The Lab reviews applications and either approves the request, declines it, or follows up for more information, reflecting a governance process that is both inclusive and selective, aligned with the Lab’s pedagogical framework and resource constraints.5,18Once approved, users sign in with CUNY Login and can access their tools from a unified Tools page, while instructors can set up class access for pilot courses by sharing invitation links that allow students to claim seats within defined course dates.5,18The FAQ elaborates on resource governance by noting a standard allowance of five dollars per month of model use per user, which is visible on the Model Access page.18This allowance constrains model usage, encourages mindful consumption, and reflects the costs associated with hosting open-weight models at university scale.12,18Since open-weight models are noted as costing far less to run than proprietary models while performing comparably on most academic tasks, the Lab’s combination of modest allowances and open-weight hosting enables broad experimentation without the financial commitments of commercial platforms.12,18The governance of access, allowances, and class-based participation is thus tightly coupled to the Lab’s infrastructural mandate: by placing clear limits and access pathways around its model gateway and doorways, the Lab ensures that AI use remains accountable and aligned with its educational mission.3,5,18### Privacy, Logging, and Data-Retention Policies

The Lab’s FAQ and tools documentation emphasize a strong privacy posture that has direct implications for both the Sandbox and the Model API.14,18Every Lab tool is tied to CUNY Login for authentication, but tools receive opaque IDs instead of direct usernames, which means backend systems process pseudonymous identifiers rather than personal accounts.18Logs are described as recording events, such as requests or errors, rather than capturing the content of prompts or responses, thereby limiting the amount of potentially sensitive information retained in the Lab’s infrastructure.18Furthermore, the Lab’s gateway configuration ensures that all requests to model providers prohibit training on user content and that prompts and outputs are not stored for later analysis, which is described as “zero data retention” across the board.14,18These privacy policies intersect with the Lab’s GitHub-based development practices: when developers push code for tools like Agent Studio or Site Studio, they embed the expectation that models will be called through a gateway that does not log or retain content, and that user interfaces should not encourage unsafe or unnecessary data sharing.3,4,14Pull requests that adjust logging mechanisms or model routing can be reviewed with an eye towards these policies, and diffs can be inspected by internal or external auditors to ensure that privacy guarantees remain intact.4,15The emphasis on open-weight models also facilitates transparency about what models can and cannot do, since their weights and training procedures can be inspected by the research community, reinforcing the Lab’s commitment to thoughtful integration of AI into academic work.3,12,18## CAIL-Pi and Model-Agnostic Workshop Infrastructure

CAIL-Pi as a Workshop-Oriented Model Provider

One of the more specialized components of the Lab’s infrastructure is the `@cuny-ai-lab/cail-pi` package published in the Pi ecosystem, which is described as a “workshop setup and model provider for Pi.”17Pi is a command-line interface and environment for running AI models, and the CAIL-Pi package is designed to configure Pi for use in CUNY AI Lab workshops by handling tasks such as verifying API keys and loading Lab models into the Pi environment.17The package documentation explains that running `npx @cuny-ai-lab/cail-pi` triggers a workshop setup sequence that verifies the key, loads the CAIL models, and ensures that participants are working with the Lab’s model stack rather than arbitrary external providers.17After setup, participants can start Pi and use commands like `/model` to select one of the CUNY AI Lab models exposed via the package, allowing them to interact with the Lab’s open-weight models through Pi’s interface.17This integration illustrates a key dimension of the Lab’s model-agnostic design: by providing a package that bridges its own Model API with an external, open-source environment like Pi, the Lab allows models to be used in a different client without sacrificing governance or privacy protections.11,14,17The package effectively acts as a doorway between Pi and the Model Registry, mapping model IDs and access keys into Pi’s configuration and enforcing the Lab’s access controls inside a workshop setting.11,17From a GitHub perspective, the CAIL-Pi project captures this logic in source code, where diffs and pull requests can refine how keys are validated, how error messages are presented, and how models are listed or selected in the Pi interface.17This repository thus documents not only a technical integration but also a pedagogical workflow for teaching command-line interaction with AI models under institutional constraints.17,7### Model-Agnostic Implications of Pi Integration

Although CAIL-Pi explicitly loads CUNY AI Lab models into Pi, the broader Pi ecosystem is designed for multi-model, multi-provider use, and the Lab’s package aligns with this model-agnostic ethos by making its models one configurable option among many.17,15In the self-hosted Agent Studio repository by pma1999, for instance, users can configure multi-provider chat that connects to OpenRouter, DeepSeek, local llama.cpp models, and other endpoints, reflecting a design where the choice of model is a parameter rather than a fixed assumption.15While this particular Agent Studio repository is independent of the CUNY AI Lab, its description mirrors the Lab’s interest in model-agnostic agent workflows, suggesting an ecosystem in which tools can be wired to different providers as long as they speak a common API dialect.11,12,15The CAIL-Pi package can therefore be understood as the Lab’s specific contribution to a broader landscape of model-agnostic tools: it inserts the Lab’s open-weight, zero-retention models into Pi while preserving Pi’s general capability to talk to other providers.17,15From the standpoint of critical AI literacy, this design is significant because it allows workshop participants to compare the behavior of CUNY-hosted models with other models in the Pi ecosystem, raising questions about performance, cost, and training data in a controlled environment.6,7,17At the same time, the package keeps the Lab’s privacy guarantees intact by routing requests through its own Model API and access keys, reminding participants that institutional governance and openness can coexist with technical flexibility.11,14,17### Pedagogical Integration: Vibe Coding and Creative Coding

The Lab’s workshop materials on creative coding and vibe coding provide context for how CAIL-Pi fits into broader pedagogical strategies.6,7,8The “Creative Coding with Generative AI” deck explains how participants can open the CAIL Sandbox, create accounts, await approval, and confirm access by posting in the chat, establishing the Sandbox as a baseline environment for experimentation.7It then refers to a GitHub project (`cuny-ai-lab/creative-coding`) as source code for the workshop, demonstrating how AI-assisted coding exercises can build directly on repository-based projects.7Similarly, the Lab’s “Vibe Coding” resources offer operational instructions for using command-line agents, setting project context, and reviewing AI-assisted development work, reinforcing the idea that coding with AI involves not just model calls but systemic thinking about workflows.4,11In blog posts, the Lab describes participants who “vibe-code” tools that provide composition students with real-time feedback on whether their paragraphs genuinely “think with” a text, reflecting a pedagogy that foregrounds reflective, critical engagement with AI outputs rather than rote automation.8Another post recounts a Fellow building a personal Pomodoro timer through AI-assisted prototyping during a workshop series and grappling with the ambivalence of automating self-regulation.8These narratives situate CAIL-Pi and related workshop infrastructure within a larger project of critical AI literacy, where tools like Pi, Sandbox, and GitHub repositories are not only technical artifacts but prompts for reflection on the role of AI in academic and everyday life.6,7,8The ability to use CAIL-Pi to connect Pi to the Lab’s models thus becomes a practical pathway for exploring these themes in a hands-on, code-centric setting.17,7## Stem Splitter: Deep Listening, Targeted Inquiry, and AI Companions

Project Overview and Functionality

The Stem Splitter repository is introduced as a “stem-separation web app for music students,” designed to accept song uploads, YouTube links, or openly licensed tracks from the Internet Archive and return isolated stems as MP3 files.1The application supports multiple separation configurations, including a two-stem split into vocals and instrumental, a four-stem split adding drums, bass, and other, and a six-stem split that further isolates guitar and piano.1These stems are then played in a synchronized in-browser mixer that offers per-stem mute controls, renameable channel labels, and shared timestamped notes, enabling users to annotate waveforms layer by layer.1The repository also describes “Listening Guy,” an AI listening companion that writes per-song listening guides and answers questions in a chat that can control the mixer itself by soloing or muting channels, jumping the playhead, and pinning notes for a class.1Listening Guy is specified as using GLM-5.2 as a base model, reflecting the Lab’s use of open-weight models for specialized pedagogical tasks.1,12This combination of audio processing, synchronous playback, annotation tools, and AI-guided listening represents a distinctive extension of the Lab’s sandbox ecosystem into discipline-specific practice.1,3Whereas the Sandbox provides general-purpose chat with models, Stem Splitter embeds models inside a web application tailored to music education, with the AI companion serving as a contextual guide that responds to the specific sonic environment presented by the stems.1The GitHub repository documents how these features are implemented, including the logic for handling uploads, invoking stem separation algorithms, synchronizing playback across channels, and wiring Listening Guy’s chat interface to mixer controls.1Diffs and pull requests in this repository can therefore capture the iterative refinement of audio separation quality, mixer usability, and AI-guide behavior as the Lab’s developers and collaborators test and adjust the tool.1,4### Pedagogical Design and Critical Listening

Stem Splitter is explicitly framed as a tool for music students, and its design suggests a pedagogy centered on deep listening, targeted inquiry, and collaborative annotation.1By allowing users to isolate vocal lines, instrumental backings, rhythm sections, and specific instruments, the tool enables focused attention on individual musical elements that might otherwise be obscured in the mix.1The shared timestamped notes feature indicates that the tool can be used in classroom settings where students and instructors annotate particular moments in the song, raising questions about harmony, rhythm, production techniques, or lyrical interpretation.1,8Listening Guy’s ability to write per-song listening guides and respond to queries about the track further situates AI not as a replacement for musical analysis but as a conversational partner that can prompt, elaborate, or challenge human observations.1,6From the perspective of critical AI literacy, this design is significant because it entwines AI guidance with sensory engagement rather than abstract text-only tasks.1,8Students must listen carefully to the stems, reflect on what they hear, and then use Listening Guy as a tool for articulating or extending their interpretations, thereby learning both musical analysis and critical evaluation of AI-generated commentary.1,6,8Since Listening Guy is based on an open-weight model (GLM-5.2), its behavior can in principle be audited or compared with other models via the Model Registry, opening the possibility of interrogating how different models frame musical experience.1,12The repository’s alignment with the Critical AI Literacy Institute’s T(h)inkering track underscores this pedagogical orientation, suggesting that Stem Splitter is not merely a convenience tool but part of a broader experiment in using AI to support reflective, critical engagement in arts education.1,8### Relationship to Sandbox, Model API, and Infrastructure

Although the Stem Splitter documentation does not explicitly detail its deployment architecture, its description as a web app with an AI listening companion suggests that it is either directly or indirectly connected to the Lab’s model gateway and open-weight stack.1,12,14If Listening Guy uses GLM-5.2 hosted through the Lab’s Model API, then the tool benefits from the same zero-retention, non-training guarantees as the Sandbox, ensuring that students’ questions and listening guides are not stored or used to retrain the model.12,14,18Even if Stem Splitter were to use external hosting for audio separation, the GitHub repository’s inclusion within the CUNY AI Lab organization indicates an alignment with the Lab’s infrastructural policies and a likely trajectory towards integration with its model-agnostic gateway.1,3,4In functional terms, Stem Splitter can be seen as an example of how the Lab’s infrastructure allows specialized tools to emerge on top of a shared model stack.3,11The Model Registry and API provide the model IDs and performance characteristics, while the Sandbox offers a general conversational environment, and tools like Stem Splitter embed models within interfaces tailored to specific academic disciplines.1,12,14Diffs and pull requests in the Stem Splitter repository can adjust how the tool calls models, whether it uses a single model or a model council pattern, and how listening guides are structured, all without requiring changes to the underlying model gateway.1,15This modularity exemplifies the Lab’s model-agnostic, infrastructure-first design philosophy, where the same set of models and policies can support diverse pedagogical applications.3,12,14## Workshop Series, Systems Mapping, and the Sandbox Pedagogy

asked to design and test system prompts for specific teaching, research, or scholarly workflows, considering the model’s role, procedure, constraints, tone, and expected output, thereby learning how prompt engineering intersects with pedagogical design.2,10The second workshop, “Building Custom Models with Knowledge Collections,” focuses on grounding custom models in participants’ own materials by research materials, datasets, and other documents.2,11Participants are encouraged to consider both technical and intellectual questions in curating useful collections, including what to include, how Skills and Tools as Reusable Procedural Components

The Lab’s documentation defines a skill as a structured procedure written in markdown or plain text, essentially a script that encodes a repeatable workflow that the model can execute in conversation.9Examples include guiding a student through a close reading, walking a researcher through a statistical analysis, or scaffolding the drafting of an argument with evidence and counterargument.9,8Tools, by contrast, are mechanisms that give models additional capabilities, such as web search, file querying, code execution, or image generation, and are typically implemented as function calls or API integrations that models can invoke when needed.9,14Together, skills and tools form modular components that can be attached to custom models in the Sandbox, allowing instructors and researchers to construct tailored AI assistants that reflect their disciplinary methods and responsibilities.2,9By separating skills (procedural scripts) from tools (external capabilities), the Lab encourages participants to think about AI workflows in terms of both internal reasoning and external operations.9,10Skills shape how models structure tasks, ask questions, and respond to users, while tools determine what actions models can take beyond text generation.9,14This separation is consistent with the Lab’s model-agnostic infrastructure: skills can be reused across different models in the Model Registry, and tools can be wired to different providers or APIs, allowing experimental combinations without locking workflows to a single model or service.12,14The GitHub repositories associated with skills and tools—even when not individually identified in the search results—serve as codebases where these components are implemented, tested, and revised through diffs and pull requests.4,11### Agent Studio in the Tools Portal

Site Studio, Media Tools, and Accessibility Pipelines

Site Studio is described as an AI-assisted academic site builder that powers the Site Studio app on the Tools Portal, helping users build academic websites, portfolios, CVs, and research pages through conversation.4,13The Links page identifies its GitHub repository as the source for this app, signaling that its code for generating pages, organizing content, and interacting with models is open for reuse and audit.4Media Tools provide transcription, image description, and document OCR, including a Media Image Description tool for generating alt-text and detailed image descriptions using vision models and a Document OCR tool for extracting text from PDFs and scanned images with high-accuracy optical character recognition.4,14The Manifold Companion project, referenced on the Links page, extends these accessibility pipelines into a multi-user OCR platform for manuscript digitization, built with Express and React and using AWS Bedrock models.4This project illustrates how the Lab’s infrastructure can be extended to specialized workflows that combine AI-based OCR with collaborative annotation and digitization tasks, supporting humanities research and archival work.4,3By publishing these tools’ source code on GitHub, the Lab allows other institutions or projects to adapt them for their own needs, while diffs and pull requests can add features such as improved language handling, metadata extraction, or integration with repository platforms.4,11The shared reliance on open-weight models and transparent hosting ensures that these tools remain aligned with the Lab’s privacy and auditability commitments.12,18## Governance Through Diffs, Pull Requests, and Open-Weight Models

Open-Source Practices in the CUNY AI Lab GitHub Organization

The GitHub repositories associated with the CUNY AI Lab exhibit core open-source practices such as public commit histories, issue tracking, and potential for pull requests from collaborators.1,4,6,7For instance, the Stem Splitter repository shows its public status, along with metadata indicating stars and forks, reflecting community engagement and potential reuse.1The Agent Studio self-hosted workspace similarly exposes its code, enabling users to clone the repository, inspect the implementation of multi-provider chat and MCP integration, and propose changes via pull requests.15Workshop materials like Creative Coding and Critical Play are also maintained in GitHub repositories, allowing participants to file issues about bugs or propose diffs that improve exercises or clarify instructions.6,7These open-source practices are essential for the Lab’s infrastructural goals because they enable shared governance of tools: instructors, students, and external developers can inspect and contribute to the code that powers Sandbox-adjacent applications, ensuring that they evolve in response to pedagogical and technical needs.3,4Diffs in these repositories document how tools change over time, whether through bug fixes, feature additions, or refactoring for clarity and maintainability.1,4,7Pull requests allow changes to be proposed, reviewed, and integrated in a controlled manner, reflecting collaborative decision-making about how AI tools should behave, what defaults they should adopt, and how they should embody the Lab’s privacy and pedagogical commitments.4,15This openness stands in contrast to commercial AI platforms, where code and model configurations are often opaque, limiting the ability of academic communities to shape tools to their values.3,12,18### Comparative Activity and Design Across Repositories

While detailed commit histories are not visible in the search results, the snapshot information suggests varied levels of activity and adoption across the Lab’s repositories.1,4,7,15Stem Splitter, for example, is described as a public repository created for a specific pedagogical track, and its features reflect careful design around music education and AI-guided listening.1Creative Coding and Critical Play repositories, by contrast, are oriented toward workshops and may see bursts of activity around event dates, as instructors update decks, add examples, or refine code in response to participant feedback.6,7,2Tools Portal repositories like Agent Studio, Site Studio, and Manifold Companion likely experience continuous development as the Lab adds capabilities, responds to bug reports, and adapts to new models in the Model Registry.3,4,11The presence of self-hosted projects like the pma1999 Agent Studio workspace indicates that similar agent concepts are being explored outside the Lab, offering opportunities for comparative analysis and cross-pollination.15,4For example, the Model Council pattern in the self-hosted Agent Studio, where several models deliberate on the same question, could inform future work in the Lab’s infrastructure if instructors or researchers desire multi-model deliberation for critical comparison.15,12Conversely, the Lab’s focus on privacy, open-weight models, and zero data retention could inspire enhancements to self-hosted tools where data governance is less stringent.12,18,15In both cases, the GitHub-based nature of these repositories allows diffs and pull requests to serve as vehicles for exchanging ideas and practices across organizational boundaries.4,15### Model-Agnostic Infrastructure and University Context

The Lab’s commitment to open-weight models, model registries, and zero data retention positions its infrastructure as model-agnostic in a deep sense: the system is designed to host and switch between multiple models without changing the fundamental shape of tools or workflows.12,13,14The Model Registry’s listing of model capabilities, context limits, and token prices enables instructors and developers to choose models pragmatically, based on task requirements and resource constraints, rather than loyalty to a particular provider.12,11The Sandbox’s integration with this registry and gateway ensures that conversational interfaces can be reconfigured to use different models as they become available, supporting experimentation and comparative pedagogy.14,2In the university context, this model-agnostic design has several implications. First, it allows the institution to avoid lock-in to proprietary ecosystems, preserving the ability to negotiate costs, audit model behavior, and adapt to changes in the AI landscape.3,12Second, it supports critical AI literacy by making model choice and configuration visible to instructors and students; participants in workshops learn not only how to use AI but which models they are using and why.2,8,11Third, it enables sustainable governance: the Lab can adjust its model offerings as budgets and pedagogical priorities shift, while the underlying tools and workflows remain compatible with the Model API and Sandbox.13,14,18Finally, by hosting tools and workshop materials on GitHub, the Lab ensures that its infrastructure can be replicated, adapted, or scrutinized by other universities, contributing to a broader movement toward open, accountable AI in higher education.3,4,11## Conclusion: Synthesis and Future Directions for Model-Agnostic AI at CUNY

The CUNY AI Lab’s GitHub organization and Sandbox ecosystem together illustrate a comprehensive approach to building AI infrastructure in a public university that foregrounds model-agnostic design, privacy, transparency, and critical pedagogy.3,13,18Through its Model Registry and CAIL Model API, the Lab exposes open-weight models with clear capabilities, context limits, and token prices, enabling programmatic access and informed model choice.11,12The Sandbox and Tools Portal—encompassing Agent Studio, Site Studio, Media Tools, and accessibility pipelines like Manifold Companion—serve as doorways to this model stack, offering tailored interfaces for chat-based exploration, research workflows, site building, and media processing while enforcing zero data retention and non-training policies.3,4,14Projects such as Stem Splitter and CAIL-Pi demonstrate how this infrastructure can be extended into discipline-specific tools and workshop integrations, supporting deep listening in music education and command-line experimentation in creative coding and vibe coding contexts.1,7,17The Lab’s Fall 2026 workshop series on system prompts, knowledge collections, skills, and tools, along with its critical play and professional development offerings, embody a pedagogy of systems mapping, where participants learn to model, configure, and critique AI workflows rather than simply consume outputs.2,6,8,9,10Skills and tools serve as reusable procedural components and external capabilities that can be wired into custom models, while Agent Studio and related repositories illustrate how agent-based workspaces can support research tasks under the Lab’s governance.3,4,15Open-source practices—transparent repositories, diffs, and pull requests—anchor this infrastructure in collaborative development, enabling instructors, students, and external developers to inspect and shape the tools they use.1,4,6,7The Lab’s insistence on open-weight models and zero data retention further aligns its technical architecture with its stated priorities of privacy and transparency, allowing it to serve as a model for other institutions seeking to build accountable AI infrastructure.12,18,13As the Lab moves beyond its pilot phase, future directions may include expanding the Model Registry, enhancing agent capabilities in Agent Studio, deepening integrations between tools and the Sandbox, and fostering more cross-institutional collaboration through its GitHub organization.3,4,11In all of these developments, the promise of model-agnostic AI infrastructure lies in the ability to treat models as configurable components within carefully designed systems, governed by institutional values and pedagogical aims rather than market pressures.3,12,18The CUNY AI Lab’s work, as documented in its GitHub repositories, Model Registry, and workshop materials, suggests that such infrastructure can support not only efficient academic workflows but also critical, reflective engagement with AI across disciplines—an outcome that is increasingly vital as universities grapple with the opportunities and risks of generative AI.2,6,8,13
