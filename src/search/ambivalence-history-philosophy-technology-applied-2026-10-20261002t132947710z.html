Ambivalent Infrastructures: Philosophy of Technology, Critical AI Literacy, and the CUNY AI Lab

The emergence of the CUNY AI Lab within the largest urban public university system in the United States offers a distinctive vantage point from which to examine the ambivalence of technology in higher education.1,2Drawing on the history and philosophy of technology, especially Andrew Feenberg’s account of technology as a contested social field and Hans Jonas’s imperative of responsibility, this report argues that CUNY’s AI initiatives instantiate a deliberately ambivalent stance toward artificial intelligence, one that neither celebrates nor rejects AI but instead builds infrastructures that keep political, ethical, and pedagogical questions open.5,13This stance is visible in the Lab’s commitment to open-weight, model‑agnostic systems, privacy‑by‑design architectures, and community‑driven governance, all of which contrast with monolithic enterprise AI deployments such as Microsoft Copilot that tend to centralize control and close off interpretive space.2,4,10At the same time, CUNY’s Critical AI Literacy Institute (CALI) and allied pedagogical projects mobilize traditions of critical education inspired by Paulo Freire to cultivate learners and instructors who can inhabit ambivalence productively, using AI as an object of inquiry and a tool in democratic, collaborative knowledge‑making rather than as a replacement for intellectual labor.17,19Taking the subject, the learner, and the institution as distinct yet entangled levels of analysis, the report shows how model‑agnostic interpretability, privacy by design, shared governance, and critical literacy practices can transform ambivalence from a symptom of technological anxiety into a principle of responsible experimentation in public higher education.2,4,5,13,17,19## Ambivalence in the History and Philosophy of Technology

Technology as a Contested Social Field

In the history and philosophy of technology, ambivalence names the refusal to treat technology as either an autonomous force of progress or a simple instrument that can be unproblematically mastered.5Andrew Feenberg’s work on the ambivalence of technology is foundational here, insisting that technologies are not neutral tools but socially constructed artefacts whose design and deployment are inseparable from political struggle and institutional power.5Feenberg emphasizes that modern technology is often represented as something one must either embrace or reject wholesale, yet in practice technical systems are internally shaped by competing social interests, values, and forms of expertise that make them sites of ongoing contestation.5He argues that this contestation requires what he calls “democratic technical politics,” where affected communities participate in shaping the norms and structures that govern technological development instead of leaving design decisions to experts, markets, or state bureaucracies.5Within this framework, ambivalence is not a weakness but an analytical strength: to acknowledge the contradictory potentials of technology is to open space for political intervention and alternative futures.5Hans Jonas offers a complementary account of ambivalence in The Imperative of Responsibility, a work that reflects deeply on the unprecedented powers unleashed by modern technological civilization.13Jonas argues that technological progress has dramatically expanded human freedom, particularly our capacity to intervene in nature and social life, while simultaneously amplifying the risks of irresponsibility and long‑term harm.13From nuclear weapons to environmental degradation, the “shadow of the Bomb” and the ecological crisis are for Jonas paradigmatic examples of how technological power can undermine the very conditions for human and non‑human life.13He contends that modernity tends to equate freedom with technical mastery and unrestrained control over nature, thereby ignoring the interwoven character of freedom and responsibility and denying the possibility that human action can have catastrophic consequences on cosmic and even transcendent scales.13Ambivalence arises in Jonas’s thought because human freedom has a twofold potential: it can sustain the adventure of creation or contribute to its ruin, and awareness of this duality must inform ethical reflection on technology.13These philosophical accounts problematize any view of technology as purely beneficent or purely destructive, insisting instead that technical artefacts are permeated by social meanings and moral stakes.5,13For Feenberg, the ambivalence of technology reflects its status as a dimension of social life akin to law, education, or corporate organization, each of which is simultaneously enabling and constraining, emancipatory and disciplinary.5For Jonas, ambivalence arises from the disproportion between human technical power and our limited foresight, creating an ethical demand to anticipate distant consequences and to restrain the abuse of newly opened capacities.13Together, these perspectives frame ambivalence as both descriptive—capturing the mixed effects of technology—and normative—calling for responsible, democratic engagement with technological change.5,13### Freedom, Responsibility, and Technological Power

Jonas’s diagnosis of modernity as a reduction of freedom to technical achievement resonates powerfully in contemporary debates about artificial intelligence.13He warns that when freedom is identified solely with increased control and efficiency, the ambivalent nature of human agency is flattened, and responsibility becomes an afterthought rather than an integral aspect of ethical life.13In his analysis, modern technology tempts societies to disavow the possibility of evil outcomes by treating power as self‑legitimating: if something can be technically done, it is assumed to be worthwhile, and limits on technical projects are portrayed as irrational or regressive.13This disavowal suppresses the recognition that freedom always includes the capacity for harm, making the demand for responsibility even more urgent as technological power expands.13Feenberg similarly criticizes narratives that present technology as a sociopolitical invariant, a force that operates independently of social choices and political conflicts.5He shows that technical designs are always relative to particular political forces and social interests, even when presented as neutral solutions.5The ambivalence of technology thus reflects not only the multiplicity of its effects but also the plurality of perspectives and stakes embodied in technical decisions.5Democratic technical politics require institutions and practices that bring these perspectives into contact, enabling publics to evaluate and transform technologies rather than passively accept them.5In this sense, ambivalence is a precondition for democracy: if technologies were simply good or bad in themselves, no space would exist for deliberation, critique, or alternative designs.5These philosophical insights are critical for understanding AI in universities, where technological infrastructures intersect with teaching, research, administration, and governance.13,5The power of AI systems to automate tasks, analyze data, and generate content amplifies both capacities and vulnerabilities, increasing efficiency while also raising questions about surveillance, bias, intellectual labor, and academic integrity.11,12An ambivalent stance inspired by Feenberg and Jonas acknowledges both the promise and the peril of AI, insisting that institutional responses cannot be reduced to either enthusiastic adoption or blanket prohibition.5,13Instead, universities must cultivate practices and structures that keep ambivalence visible, inviting faculty, students, and staff to participate in shaping AI’s role in academic life.1,2,4## The Contemporary University and AI: The CUNY Context

Systemwide AI Initiatives in a Public University

The City University of New York (CUNY) is the largest urban public university system in the United States, encompassing 26 campuses that serve a highly diverse student body.1Recognizing the growing significance of artificial intelligence for teaching, learning, and workforce development, CUNY has launched a broad set of AI‑related initiatives across these campuses.1These initiatives include campus‑level AI task forces, capacity‑building incubators, literacy and workforce development programs, industry partnerships, and applied research projects in multiple disciplines.1The system’s AI Academic Hub aggregates these efforts and articulates guiding principles for AI use in the university, positioning AI as a set of tools that should augment rather than supplant human intelligence and academic judgment.3This systemwide approach already reflects an ambivalent posture: AI is treated as both opportunity and risk, requiring coordinated guidance and localized experimentation.1,3Within this ecosystem, CUNY has established an AI Innovation Fund that provides support for over one hundred campus‑led AI initiatives, with public funding aimed at strengthening New York’s role in AI development and application.1The Fund’s structure foregrounds ambivalence by distributing resources across varied projects rather than converging on a single standardized solution, thereby encouraging multiple approaches to AI that can be evaluated and revised in light of experience.1This dispersion aligns with Feenberg’s emphasis on technology as a site of social struggle, since different campuses and departments can explore context‑specific ways of integrating AI while participating in system‑level conversations about values and risks.5,1It also aligns with Jonas’s call for responsible experimentation, where the expansion of technical power is accompanied by attention to future consequences and ethical constraints.13,1The AI Academic Hub stresses human‑centered principles, including fairness, transparency, and accountability, as central to AI use in teaching and administration.3These principles recognize the ambivalence of AI’s impact on students and faculty, acknowledging that algorithmic tools can deepen inequities if not designed and governed carefully.3The hub’s guidance frames AI as supporting human judgment rather than replacing it, highlighting the need to maintain critical engagement and to avoid outsourcing core academic functions to opaque systems.3In this context, the CUNY AI Lab at the Graduate Center emerges as a key institutional experiment in building AI infrastructure that embodies these ambivalent, human‑centered commitments.2,4### The CUNY AI Lab as Institutional Experiment

The CUNY AI Lab (CAIL) is a faculty‑ and staff‑led initiative based at the CUNY Graduate Center that develops and maintains AI infrastructure for teaching, research, and academic work across the university system.2,4Rather than functioning as a vendor or a purely technical unit, the Lab positions itself as a collaborative incubator where academic workers can experiment with AI tools under conditions that prioritize privacy, transparency, and critical literacy.2,4Its work includes the CAIL Sandbox, a platform where CUNY users can engage with a curated set of models, consult a public Model Registry that details current API offerings, and configure tools for research and classroom use.2The Sandbox is designed as a zero‑retention environment, meaning that providers are chosen so that user data is not stored or used for model training, and interactions are kept as ephemeral as possible.4This design choice materializes ambivalence at the infrastructural level: AI is made accessible, but within strict constraints that foreground user control and data protection.2,4Beyond the Sandbox, CAIL operates a Tools Portal that supports research workflows, academic publishing, and direct model access, alongside specialized environments such as Agent Studio for research tasks involving sources, data, files, drafts, and visualizations.2Site Studio enables the construction of academic websites, while Media Tools provide transcription, optical character recognition, and image description, and additional services help make PDFs accessible or assist in drafting AI‑use disclosure statements.4Through these tools, the Lab embeds AI into existing academic practices, not as a replacement for scholarly and pedagogical labor but as a configurable set of supports that can be reshaped as needs evolve.2,4The Model Registry provides detailed information about model capabilities, context limits, and token pricing, enabling users to make informed choices and to compare models based on technical and economic criteria.2These features collectively establish the Lab as an infrastructure for ambivalent engagement: users are invited to explore AI, but are also equipped to question, evaluate, and adapt the systems they use.2,4The Lab’s guiding principles articulate this ethos explicitly: it commits to privacy by design, transparency through open‑weight models that can be audited, environmental accountability via visibility into computational costs, and criticality, defined as AI literacy over AI dependency.4These principles respond to the philosophical concerns raised by Feenberg and Jonas by building institutional safeguards that temper technological freedom with responsibility and public scrutiny.5,13,4They also differentiate CAIL from monolithic enterprise frameworks that prioritize scale and integration over legibility and community control.4,10The Lab draws inspiration from initiatives such as CUNY’s Academic Commons and Manifold Scholarship platforms, which reflect longstanding commitments to accessible, scalable digital infrastructure created for and with faculty, students, and staff.2In this way, CAIL situates AI within a broader history of public‑oriented digital projects at CUNY, reinforcing the ambivalent view of technology as simultaneously enabling and contestable.2,5## Model‑Agnosticism, Explainability, and Ambivalent Legibility

Model‑Agnostic Interpretability: LIME and SHAP

One of the central technical themes in contemporary debates about AI is model‑agnostic interpretability, an approach that treats trained models as black boxes and seeks to explain their predictions without relying on specific architectural details.11,12Model‑agnostic methods are described as such because their implementation does not depend on the internal structure of the model; they use only input data and output predictions to infer patterns of behavior.11In contrast, model‑specific methods exploit particular features of a given architecture, such as gradients in deep neural networks, tying explanations to the design of the underlying system.11Model‑agnostic techniques are versatile: they can be applied to any type of model—linear, tree‑based, neural, or ensemble—and to diverse data modalities including text, images, and tabular datasets.11,12Their flexibility has made them central to the field of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), which seeks to render complex models intelligible to practitioners and stakeholders.12Two of the most widely used model‑agnostic frameworks are Local Interpretable Model‑Agnostic Explanations (LIME) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).11,12LIME operates by locally approximating the behavior of a black‑box classifier or regressor with an interpretable surrogate model, such as a sparse linear model, near a specific data point.11,12It perturbs the input around that point, generates multiple synthetic samples, and observes how the model’s predictions change, thereby identifying which features are most influential for that particular prediction.11Because LIME focuses on local neighborhoods in the input space, it provides detailed explanations for individual decisions rather than a global picture of model behavior.11,12Its model‑agnostic character allows it to be deployed across domains, including image classification, text analysis, and structured data, offering textual or visual explanations that highlight relevant features.11,12SHAP, by contrast, is grounded in cooperative game theory and uses Shapley values to attribute the contribution of each feature to a model’s output.11,12It considers all possible coalitions of features and calculates how the inclusion of a given feature affects the prediction, averaging across coalitions to obtain a measure of importance.11SHAP can provide both local explanations, for single instances, and global summaries, showing how features influence the model across a dataset.12Like LIME, SHAP is post‑hoc and model‑agnostic, meaning it can be applied after training and without access to internal parameters, although certain implementations exploit model‑specific structures to improve computational efficiency.11,12In comparative analyses, LIME is often characterized as prioritizing local fidelity and interpretability, while SHAP offers a unified framework that balances local and global insights with a stronger theoretical foundation.12Together, these methods exemplify the ambivalence of interpretability: they increase legibility and accountability, yet they also risk simplifying complex behaviors into heuristics that may be misunderstood or overtrusted.11,12To illustrate the differences between these frameworks, consider the following schematic comparison, which draws on systematic reviews but reformulates the distinctions:

| Aspect | LIME | SHAP |
|-------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Primary scope | Local explanations for individual predictions | Local and global explanations across predictions |
| Theoretical foundation | Approximate surrogate modeling | Game‑theoretic Shapley values |
| Model dependence | Model‑agnostic post‑hoc | Model‑agnostic, with model‑specific optimizations |
| Typical explanation formats | Textual and visual feature importance | Numeric and visual feature attributions |
| Data types supported | Text, image, tabular | Text, image, tabular |

This table, based on existing overviews of XAI methods, underscores how both LIME and SHAP seek to bridge the opacity of complex models and the need for human interpretability.11,12Their model‑agnosticism mirrors a broader institutional desire to treat AI systems as configurable components rather than fixed infrastructures, allowing universities and other organizations to interrogate and adapt models within diverse contexts.11,12### Model‑Agnosticism and the CUNY AI Lab

The CUNY AI Lab’s emphasis on open‑weight models and a public Model Registry reflects an institutional adoption of model‑agnostic principles at the infrastructural level.2By cataloging models according to their capabilities, context limits, and token prices, and by enabling users to compare these attributes, the Lab treats models as interchangeable modules in a larger ecosystem of tools rather than as monolithic platforms.2This stance echoes the logic of model‑agnostic interpretability: just as LIME and SHAP subject black‑box models to analysis without depending on their architectures, the Lab seeks to make AI systems legible and evaluable without committing to a single vendor‑specific stack.2,11,12Open‑weight models, which allow inspection of parameters and training procedures, further enhance this legibility by enabling audit and critique of model behavior, bias, and environmental cost.4In this sense, model‑agnosticism becomes an institutional design principle, promoting flexibility, transparency, and contestability in the choice and deployment of AI tools.2,4The Lab’s Sandbox environment aligns with this model‑agnostic philosophy by providing a zero‑retention chat interface to curated models that can be switched, combined, or replaced as needed.2,4Because user interactions are not stored or used to train models, the Sandbox decouples AI experimentation from data extraction, reducing the lock‑in that often accompanies proprietary systems.4Faculty, staff, and students can prototype custom models for teaching, research, and critique, treating AI as a configurable resource rather than an immutable service.4This modularity preserves ambivalence by making it easier to question, adjust, or abandon particular models in response to pedagogical, ethical, or political concerns, rather than being constrained by long‑term contracts or technical dependencies.2,4It also supports Feenberg’s democratic technical politics, since communities can compare alternatives and advocate for specific models based on shared criteria of fairness, accuracy, and environmental impact.5,4At the same time, the use of model‑agnostic explainability tools is itself ambivalent.11,12While LIME and SHAP can reveal patterns of feature importance and decision logic, their explanations depend on assumptions about local linearity or coalition effects that may not capture all aspects of model behavior.11,12If users treat these explanations as definitive rather than approximate, interpretability can falsely reassure rather than genuinely inform, masking deeper issues of bias or misuse.11,12The CUNY AI Lab’s commitment to critical AI literacy over dependency seeks to address this risk by encouraging users to develop judgment about AI tools, including an understanding of the limits of model‑agnostic explanations.4,17Ambivalence thus becomes a safeguard against overconfidence: legibility is valued, but not at the cost of forgetting that models remain complex artefacts embedded in social and institutional contexts.5,11,12## Privacy by Design and Institutional Responsibility

Privacy by Design: Principles and Practices

Privacy by design (PbD) is an approach to systems engineering that integrates data protection and user privacy into the architecture of technologies from the outset rather than treating them as add‑on features.15Initially developed by Ann Cavoukian and formalized in a joint report on privacy‑enhancing technologies in the 1990s, PbD is structured around seven foundational principles.15It emphasizes a proactive, preventative orientation, arguing that systems should anticipate privacy risks and mitigate them before they materialize rather than merely responding to breaches.15It insists that privacy should be the default setting, so that users do not have to take special actions to protect their data; collection, retention, and use of personal information must be bounded by clearly specified purposes and minimized as much as possible.15Privacy must be embedded directly into design, ensuring that technical and organizational measures jointly produce robust end‑to‑end security across the full lifecycle of data.15PbD also articulates a “positive‑sum” approach, rejecting the idea that privacy and functionality are in zero‑sum conflict; instead, it argues that systems can be designed to optimize both simultaneously through careful engineering and governance.15Visibility and transparency are central: data practices should be open and comprehensible to users and oversight bodies, enabling accountability and trust.15Finally, PbD calls for user‑centric design, respecting individuals’ needs and rights and ensuring that interfaces and policies are understandable and accessible.15These principles transform privacy from a compliance issue into a normative design goal, demanding that data minimization, purpose specification, and secure handling be implemented as core technical features.15They resonate strongly with Jonas’s insistence that modern technological power must be tempered by responsibility and foresight, particularly when it comes to irreversible harms and the vulnerability of individuals and communities.13,15In practice, PbD requires detailed attention to how data is collected, processed, and stored.15Systems must clearly communicate the purposes for which data is used, limit collection to what is strictly necessary, anonymize or pseudonymize data where possible, and implement security measures that protect information throughout its lifecycle.15They must also ensure that by default, personal data is not exposed to an indefinite number of actors or used beyond the original intent of its collection.15These requirements challenge institutions to rethink technical architecture and governance structures, aligning them with ethical commitments to user autonomy and protection.15,13When applied to AI systems, privacy by design confronts the common practice of training models on large amounts of user data, insisting instead on data minimization, explicit consent, and transparency about how information contributes to model behavior.15,11### Privacy by Design in the CUNY AI Lab and CUNY’s AI Governance

The CUNY AI Lab explicitly adopts privacy by design as a guiding principle, committing to the use of providers that do not retain or train on user data.4Its Sandbox environment is structured around zero data retention, with interactions designed to be ephemeral and not incorporated into ongoing model development.4This approach reflects PbD’s insistence on default privacy and data minimization: users can experiment with AI tools without worrying that their queries, drafts, or research materials will be stored or repurposed beyond their immediate session.4,15It also embodies user‑centric design by placing control over data firmly in the hands of the academic community, rather than outsourcing storage and analysis to opaque external platforms.4,15In conjunction with open‑weight models that can be audited, this privacy‑by‑design architecture enables critical scrutiny of model training data and behavior, further aligning with PbD’s emphasis on transparency and accountability.4,15At the system level, CUNY’s AI Academic Hub underscores the importance of human‑centered principles, fairness, and transparency in AI use, implicitly connecting these values to privacy and data protection.3By framing AI as a tool that should augment rather than replace human intelligence, the Hub signals that data practices must support rather than undermine academic freedom, integrity, and trust.3York College’s AI Vision and Guidelines, developed within the CUNY system, make these connections explicit by prohibiting the use of confidential information such as student records or personally identifiable data in public AI systems and requiring adherence to institutional data classification standards.10The guidelines stress that institutional responsibilities include restricting the entry of sensitive information into public AI tools and distinguishing between public platforms and enterprise AI systems that operate under stronger protections and governance frameworks.10They describe YorkGPT as an internally developed, protected AI system that ensures data security through intranet or VPN requirements, access control, and physical separation, with tiered access based on data sensitivity.10This focus on internal infrastructures and access control reflects PbD’s call for end‑to‑end security and lifecycle protection of data.10,15These privacy‑by‑design practices respond to Jonas’s warning that modern technology can expand the scope of irresponsible action, especially when data is collected and used without adequate foresight.13By limiting data flows into public AI tools and building protected systems such as YorkGPT and CAIL’s Sandbox, CUNY institutions attempt to temper technical freedom with responsibility, ensuring that AI experimentation does not compromise student and faculty privacy.4,10,13At the same time, the contrast with enterprise platforms such as Microsoft Copilot, which York’s guidelines discuss in relation to an enterprise data protection statement, reveals the ambivalence of institutional choices.10Enterprise AI promises robust security and compliance, but it also centralizes control and standardizes data practices according to vendor priorities, potentially reducing local autonomy and transparency.10,5CUNY’s combination of internal AI infrastructures, privacy‑by‑design policies, and critical literacy initiatives can be read as an attempt to hold this ambivalence open, leveraging some benefits of enterprise frameworks while maintaining space for community‑driven alternatives.2,4,10,15## Shared Governance and Community‑Driven AI Infrastructures

Faculty‑ and Staff‑Led AI Infrastructures

The governance structure of the CUNY AI Lab is central to its ambivalent stance toward technology, as it is explicitly described as a faculty‑ and staff‑led initiative rather than a top‑down administrative or vendor‑controlled project.2,4This leadership composition means that the people who design and maintain the Lab’s tools are themselves embedded in teaching and research, experiencing firsthand how AI affects academic labor and student learning.2The Lab invites collaboration from faculty, researchers, and students across CUNY, welcoming participation in tool development, documentation, training, and events.2This openness embodies Feenberg’s notion of democratic technical politics, where those affected by technology contribute to shaping its design and governance.5,2It also resonates with Freirean ideas of collective learning and critical dialogue, fostering communities of practice that can interrogate AI’s role in knowledge production and institutional life.19,17The Lab’s work includes publishing Sandbox documentation, guides, and tutorials; hosting events; and sharing open‑source projects on platforms such as GitHub for reuse and adaptation.2These practices make the Lab’s tools and design choices legible to the wider community, enabling feedback, critique, and contribution.2As a result, AI integration at CUNY is not limited to administrative edicts or proprietary deployments; instead, it develops through iterative collaboration between technologists, educators, librarians, and students.2,4This shared governance structure supports ambivalence by resisting closure around a single narrative of AI—whether celebratory or alarmist—and instead sustaining a plurality of perspectives that can inform technical and pedagogical decisions.5,2It also creates institutional spaces where the risks and benefits of AI can be weighed collectively, aligning with Jonas’s imperative to consider long‑term consequences and moral obligations when deploying powerful technologies.13,2Systemwide, CUNY’s AI Academic Hub and AI Innovation Fund further distribute governance by supporting campus‑led initiatives and encouraging departments to articulate discipline‑specific guidelines.1,3Rather than imposing a uniform AI policy across all programs, the Hub and Fund recognize that different fields have distinct epistemologies, methodologies, and ethical concerns that shape how AI should be used.1,3This recognition fosters what Freire would describe as problematization: disciplines are invited to pose questions about AI in their own terms, engaging students and faculty in acts of knowing that interrogate how AI recalibrates knowledge and power.19,17Consequently, governance is not merely procedural but also pedagogical, as decisions about AI use become opportunities for critical education.3,17,19### York College and Internal AI Systems

York College’s AI Vision and Guidelines exemplify how shared governance and internal infrastructure can operate within a CUNY campus.10The document emphasizes that AI is increasingly shaping educational, research, and administrative activities in higher education and that AI proficiency has become a rapidly accelerating competency for future employment.10At the same time, it calls on the institution to encourage responsible, pedagogically appropriate exploration of AI tools and to support AI literacy through mentored opportunities for students, faculty, and staff.10Faculty retain the authority to determine how AI technologies may be used in their courses, but these decisions must align with institutional policies, discipline‑based standards, and clearly articulated learning objectives.10Expectations regarding AI use and disclosure are to be communicated in syllabi and assignment instructions, and instructors are encouraged to model best practices such as usage disclosure, data security, and critical engagement.10This structure situates AI governance at the intersection of departmental autonomy, institutional policy, and pedagogical responsibility.10York’s guidelines also specify student responsibilities, including understanding and respecting course policies regarding AI use, verifying AI‑generated information, ensuring that submitted work reflects genuine intellectual engagement, and disclosing AI use when required.10Submitting AI‑generated work without appropriate disclosure may constitute academic misconduct under existing CUNY policies.10These norms connect AI use to longstanding values of academic integrity, framing AI as a tool that must be integrated into ethical practices rather than as a shortcut that bypasses learning.10In terms of data privacy and security, the guidelines reiterate institutional responsibilities such as adherence to CUNY data classification standards, restrictions on entering confidential information into public AI tools, and recognition of the distinctions between public AI platforms and enterprise AI systems.10YorkGPT, an internally developed and protected AI system, is presented as a solution that ensures data security through technical and organizational controls while providing tiered access based on data sensitivity.10By designing and governing its own AI system, York College extends shared governance into technical architecture, allowing local decisions about data and access to reflect community values rather than vendor imperatives.10,5This blending of internal infrastructure, faculty autonomy, student responsibilities, and institutional policy illustrates how shared governance can operationalize ambivalence.10AI is neither simply mandated nor forbidden; it is permitted, encouraged, and constrained in ways that invite critical engagement and ethical reflection.10,19The existence of YorkGPT alongside references to enterprise systems such as Copilot underscores that institutions face choices between adopting external platforms and building local solutions, each with different implications for autonomy, legibility, and control.10,5Shared governance creates the conditions under which these choices can be debated and revised, ensuring that AI deployment remains a dynamic, contested process rather than a one‑time technical decision.5,10## Critical AI Literacy: Ambivalent Subjects and Learners

The Critical AI Literacy Institute (CALI)

The CUNY Graduate Center’s Teaching and Learning Center has launched a major initiative, supported by a $1 million grant from Google.org, to advance critical AI literacy in higher education.17This project, titled Disciplinary Frameworks for Critical AI Literacy, is designed to help CUNY faculty and graduate student instructors explore how generative AI affects their scholarly fields and to develop curricular strategies that enable undergraduates to engage critically with AI.17Through the Critical AI Literacy Institute (CALI), faculty members from across disciplines are recruited to collaborate on designing, testing, and refining educational resources related to AI.17The initiative unfolds over three years, beginning with seminars that build foundational knowledge in AI literacy, followed by intensive summer institutes where teaching materials are co‑developed in disciplinary clusters, then tested in courses and evaluated through research on their impact.17Over time, additional groups of faculty and graduate instructors join, expanding the project’s reach and improving curricula through iterative refinement.17One of the key features of CALI is its emphasis on creating collaborative communities of practice rather than isolated efforts.17Faculty from different disciplines work together to develop AI curricula that reflect their field’s epistemologies and methodologies while benefiting from interdisciplinary insights.17This collaboration fosters shared governance over AI literacy, as disciplinary and cross‑disciplinary perspectives jointly shape educational materials.17The project culminates in the creation of discipline‑based guidance that academic programs may incorporate into curricular conversations, as well as white papers that offer recommendations on implementing AI literacy across different academic programs.17The resources developed through CALI are shared widely as open educational resources, enabling other institutions and educators to learn from CUNY’s work and adapt it to their own contexts.17By framing AI literacy as a disciplinary and public endeavor, CALI integrates ambivalence into pedagogy: AI is both object of study and tool, both source of excitement and concern, and students are invited to inhabit this tension critically.17,19### Freirean Critical Education and Generative AI

CALI’s approach is informed by traditions of critical education associated with Paulo Freire, whose work on critical literacy and learner autonomy remains influential in the analysis of generative AI in educational contexts.19Freire understands critical education as a process of problematization, critical dialogue, and acts of knowing, where learners collectively interrogate social reality and seek democratic transformation.19Learning autonomy, in this framework, is not simply individual freedom but a socially constructed, collective endeavor rooted in praxis—action and reflection—that empowers students to become active citizens.19An article on critical education and generative AI argues that Gen‑AI technological freedom is a dubious virtue when it is framed as unrestrained access to automated content generation, because it offers little to the development of critical learning and reasoning capacities if not embedded in dialogical, reflective practices.19The authors contend that Freire’s conception of intellectual freedom as grounded in ethical commitments and rigorous inquiry can serve as a counterpoint to the instrumental view of AI as a mere productivity tool.19In this Freirean perspective, ambivalence toward AI is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be embraced in critical pedagogy.19Generative AI can facilitate creative experimentation and support language learning, but it can also undermine intellectual labor if used to outsource thinking and writing without reflection.19Critical AI literacy therefore aims to help students evaluate when AI use aligns with their values and learning needs and when it risks eroding autonomy and social connection.19,17It emphasizes active citizenship, intellectual empowerment, and the collectivization of learning via problematization, encouraging students to question how AI systems are built, whose interests they serve, and how they might reinforce or challenge existing power structures.19Ambivalence becomes pedagogical: recognizing the conflicting potentials of AI is necessary for responsible freedom in its use.19,13The CUNY AI Lab’s guiding principle of “criticality,” defined as AI literacy over AI dependency, aligns closely with this Freirean orientation.4By prioritizing literacy and judgment, the Lab encourages users to understand AI tools, their strengths and weaknesses, and their ethical implications, rather than simply relying on them as authoritative sources or automatic solutions.4,19Through documentation, training, and events, CAIL supports teaching and experimentation that foregrounds critical engagement, helping faculty and students integrate AI into their work while maintaining space for skepticism and critique.2,4,17CALI extends this work into the curriculum, building structures for sustained, discipline‑specific reflection on AI that challenges monolithic narratives of technological inevitability.17,19Together, these initiatives cultivate ambivalent subjects and learners who can navigate AI’s contradictory potentials with awareness and responsibility.13,17,19## Ambivalence at the Institutional Level: Public Universities Between Empowerment and Capture

Technology, Public Institutions, and Democratic Responsibility

Public universities such as CUNY occupy a distinctive position in the landscape of technological change.1They are charged with expanding access to education, producing research, and preparing students for participation in civic and economic life, all while operating under financial constraints and public scrutiny.1The rise of AI intensifies these responsibilities by creating new demands for digital infrastructures, data governance, and pedagogical innovation.1,3Feenberg’s and Jonas’s insights highlight that institutions cannot simply adopt AI as a neutral tool; they must recognize its ambivalent character and the political stakes of technical decisions.5,13For Feenberg, universities are part of the broader social process in which technology is designed and deployed; their choices about AI platforms, governance structures, and curricula contribute to the shaping of technological futures.5For Jonas, the expansion of institutional technological power, including the capacity to analyze student data or automate decision‑making, requires heightened responsibility to avoid harms that may be difficult to foresee but profound in their impact.13CUNY’s AI ecosystem reflects these tensions.1,3The AI Academic Hub articulates human‑centered principles and emphasizes fairness and transparency, acknowledging that AI can exacerbate inequalities if not carefully managed.3The AI Innovation Fund supports campus‑led projects, distributing technical experimentation across contexts and avoiding a single, centralized AI solution.1The CUNY AI Lab builds privacy‑by‑design, open‑weight, and model‑agnostic infrastructures that prioritize legibility and community control.2,4York College develops its own internal AI system, YorkGPT, under strict data security and tiered access regimes.10CALI mobilizes pedagogical resources to help disciplines rethink AI’s role in knowledge production.17These initiatives collectively position CUNY as a site where AI’s ambivalence is institutionally acknowledged and operationalized through diverse, community‑driven approaches.1,2,3,4,10,17Yet the pressures toward technological capture remain.5Enterprise platforms such as Microsoft Copilot, discussed in York’s guidelines in relation to enterprise data protection frameworks, offer integrated solutions that promise efficiency, consistency, and compliance with external regulations.10They can streamline administrative processes and provide powerful tools for faculty and students, but they also embed institutional data and practices into vendor‑controlled ecosystems.10,5This embedding can limit local capacity to interrogate, adapt, or exit from specific technologies, undermining democratic technical politics.5It may also encourage an instrumental view of AI, where the primary question is how to implement existing solutions rather than whether alternative designs would better align with public values and responsibilities.5,19Ambivalence at the institutional level thus involves not only recognizing the pros and cons of AI tools but also understanding the structural implications of choosing between internal infrastructures and monolithic enterprise stacks.5,10### YorkGPT, Copilot, and Institutional Choice

York College’s dual attention to YorkGPT and enterprise systems like Copilot illustrates the concrete stakes of institutional ambivalence.10YorkGPT is described as an internally developed and protected AI system that operates within the college’s intranet or VPN, uses access control and group assignment, and maintains physical separation to ensure data security.10It offers tiered access based on data sensitivity, allowing certain users and groups to work with sensitive and confidential academic information under strict limitations.10This design reflects privacy‑by‑design principles and demonstrates institutional commitment to controlling data flows, protecting student records, and aligning AI use with local policies and values.10,15It gives the college leverage over how models are configured, which datasets they draw upon, and how outputs are integrated into workflows, preserving space for community governance and adaptation.10,5By contrast, Copilot is referenced in relation to Microsoft’s enterprise data protection statement, which outlines how enterprise AI systems protect organizational data within vendor‑managed environments.10While such statements are important for compliance and technical security, they situate decision‑making about data handling within the vendor’s architecture and policies.10Institutions adopting Copilot rely on external guarantees and may have limited visibility into model training, data flows, and algorithmic changes.10,15The standardization and scale of enterprise platforms can make it harder for universities to maintain ambivalent, critical stances toward AI, as reliance on vendor solutions may encourage acceptance of default configurations and discourage local experimentation with alternative designs.5,10In Feenberg’s terms, enterprise stacks can treat technology as a sociopolitical invariant, narrowing the field of technical politics and reducing opportunities for democratic intervention.5,10York’s guidelines, however, do not simply endorse or reject Copilot; they encourage responsible use that respects data classification standards and distinguishes between public and enterprise AI systems.10This nuanced approach exemplifies institutional ambivalence: enterprise AI is acknowledged as powerful and potentially valuable, yet its adoption is framed within strong conditions and complemented by internal solutions like YorkGPT.10CUNY’s broader ecosystem, including CAIL and CALI, reinforces this ambivalence by providing alternative infrastructures and pedagogical frameworks that keep AI open to critique and redesign.2,4,17Jonas’s imperative of responsibility remains crucial here: as institutions expand their use of AI, they must anticipate the long‑term implications of vendor dependence and ensure that technological choices do not undermine academic freedom, privacy, or democratic participation.13,10## Monolithic Enterprise Frameworks and the Erosion of Ambivalence

Centralization, Efficiency, and Closure

Monolithic enterprise AI frameworks promise to resolve institutional ambivalence by offering integrated, turnkey solutions that streamline workflows and centralize data management.10They often present AI as a set of services that can be easily deployed across an organization, with vendor‑provided assurances about security, compliance, and performance.10From the perspective of administrators, such frameworks can appear to reduce complexity and risk, as they bundle multiple functionalities—document generation, data analysis, communication—into a single platform with unified governance.10This centralization, however, tends to close off alternative pathways, making it more difficult for institutions to experiment with different models, architectures, or governance structures.5,10It can also reduce the visibility of technical details, as proprietary systems limit access to model internals and training data, undermining transparency and critical scrutiny.11,15Feenberg’s critique of technological autonomy is instructive here.5Enterprise frameworks may portray their design choices as purely technical, oriented toward efficiency and reliability, but these choices inevitably reflect specific social interests, including the commercial goals of vendors and the priorities of institutional leadership.5By reifying these choices as neutral, monolithic stacks can discourage debate over alternative designs that might better serve public values such as equity, privacy, and democratic participation.5,10Jonas’s emphasis on the ambivalent power of technology further suggests that centralization increases the stakes of responsibility: when AI systems become deeply integrated into institutional workflows, errors, biases, or unethical uses can have wide‑ranging and long‑lasting consequences.13,10If governance is outsourced to vendors, institutions may find themselves ill‑equipped to anticipate and mitigate these harms.13,15From a pedagogical standpoint, monolithic frameworks risk eroding critical AI literacy by normalizing AI as a ubiquitous, unquestioned utility.19When tools like Copilot are integrated into standard office suites and learning management systems, students and faculty may come to view AI outputs as automatically trustworthy or necessary, reducing the impetus to interrogate how models work and whose interests they serve.19,10Freire’s conception of critical education as fostering problematization, dialogue, and acts of knowing is undermined when AI is treated as a background infrastructure rather than a foregrounded object of inquiry.19Learners may be encouraged to use AI to complete assignments more efficiently rather than to engage in collaborative reflection on AI’s role in shaping knowledge and power.19,17Ambivalence, in this context, is suppressed: AI becomes an unquestioned good, and the space for critical hesitation or ethical doubt is diminished.5,19### Infrastructures that Preserve Ambivalence

The CUNY AI Lab offers an alternative model by building infrastructures that preserve and even institutionalize ambivalence.2,4Its use of open‑weight models allows users and researchers to examine how models are trained and to audit their behavior for bias, error, and environmental impact.4,11The Model Registry presents models as comparable options with different capabilities and costs, encouraging users to make explicit choices rather than defaulting to a single platform.2The Sandbox’s zero‑retention design and emphasis on privacy‑by‑design resist the data‑extractive logic that often underpins enterprise AI, thereby decoupling experimentation from surveillance and commodification.4,15By supporting documentation, training, and events that focus on critical AI literacy, CAIL treats technology as an object of public discourse rather than a hidden infrastructure.2,4,17YorkGPT similarly exemplifies an infrastructure that preserves ambivalence, as it is developed internally with explicit attention to data classification, tiered access, and institutional values.10Its existence alongside references to Copilot suggests that campuses can choose to blend internal and external solutions, maintaining local control over some aspects of AI while negotiating cautious engagement with vendor services.10CALI reinforces this by turning AI into a curricular object, inviting disciplines to problematize AI and develop teaching materials that foreground its impacts on learning, knowledge production, the environment, and institutions.17Together, these initiatives create an ecosystem where AI is both used and questioned, where students and faculty can experience its benefits while also confronting its risks and limitations.2,4,10,17,19Such infrastructures embody Feenberg’s democratic technical politics by enabling communities to participate in decisions about model choice, data handling, and pedagogical integration.5,2,10,17They respond to Jonas’s imperative of responsibility by recognizing that institutional deployments of AI carry long‑term consequences and must be designed with ethical foresight.13,4,10They align with Freirean critical education by fostering spaces for dialogue and acts of knowing that treat AI as a site of intellectual labor and democratic transformation.19,17In all these respects, the CUNY AI Lab and associated initiatives demonstrate how ambivalence can be institutionalized as a method rather than merely endured as a symptom of technological anxiety.5,13,17,19## Ambivalence as Method: Designing AI Practices that Keep Questions Open

Subject‑Level Ambivalence: The Academic User

At the level of the individual subject—the faculty member, student, or staff person—ambivalence toward AI arises from the simultaneous recognition of its power and its limitations.19,17AI tools can assist in drafting, translation, summarization, data analysis, and accessibility, offering significant support for research and learning.2,4,11Yet they can also mislead through hallucinated content, embed biases present in training data, and tempt users to outsource cognitive work and ethical judgment.11,12,19Critical AI literacy seeks to cultivate subjects who can navigate this ambivalence responsibly, using AI when it aligns with their values and learning goals and abstaining when it threatens intellectual labor or privacy.17,19CALI’s seminars and institutes provide faculty and graduate instructors with opportunities to reflect on how AI intersects with their disciplines, encouraging them to develop teaching materials that make these tensions explicit.17CAIL’s documentation and training similarly help users understand model behavior, data policies, and ethical considerations, fostering judgment rather than passive reliance.2,4Privacy‑by‑design infrastructures such as the Sandbox support subject‑level ambivalence by enabling experimentation without sacrificing control over data.4,15Users can test models, refine prompts, and explore AI‑assisted workflows knowing that their interactions are not being stored or used to train future systems.4This knowledge reduces the fear that using AI will automatically participate in surveillance or commodification, while still leaving room for skepticism and critique.4,15Model‑agnostic interpretability tools, whether directly accessible or conceptually integrated into training, help users see how models make decisions, further empowering them to question outputs and avoid overtrust.11,12Through these combined practices, academic subjects at CUNY can inhabit ambivalence as a reflective stance, neither fully embracing nor fully rejecting AI but using it as an object of ongoing inquiry.5,13,17,19### Learner‑Level Ambivalence: Students and Critical Education

At the learner level, ambivalence is tied to questions of autonomy, intellectual labor, and democratic citizenship.19Students may experience AI as both a helpful resource and a potential shortcut that diminishes their engagement with course materials.19,10York’s guidelines emphasize that submitted work must reflect students’ own analytical and interpretive work and that AI should support, not substitute, their intellectual engagement.10They require students to understand and respect course policies on AI use, verify AI‑generated information, and disclose AI use when authorized, linking AI practices to academic integrity and ethical responsibility.10CALI extends this by designing curricula that help undergraduates reflect on AI’s impact on learning and knowledge production, engaging them in disciplinary conversations about appropriate and inappropriate uses.17Freirean critical education suggests that such reflection is essential for developing responsible freedom, where learners are empowered to act but also aware of the ethical stakes of their actions.19Ambivalence at the learner level is thus an educational resource rather than a problem to be eliminated.19By encountering AI as something that can both facilitate and undermine their learning, students are invited to practice critical thinking, weigh trade‑offs, and participate in dialogues about the future of education.19,17Assignments that require students to analyze AI outputs, compare them with scholarly sources, or reflect on their own use of AI in writing and research can turn ambivalence into acts of knowing.19,17Such practices align with Feenberg’s and Jonas’s insistence that technology must be integrated into democratic processes of judgment and responsibility.5,13AI becomes a topic for collective discussion—not only about its technical features but also about its role in shaping knowledge, labor, and social relations.17,19### Institutional‑Level Ambivalence: Governance, Infrastructure, and Policy

At the institutional level, ambivalence manifests in decisions about AI governance, infrastructure, and policy.1,3,10CUNY’s systemwide AI initiatives, including the AI Academic Hub, the AI Innovation Fund, CAIL, YorkGPT, and CALI, embody a strategy of distributed experimentation and guidance rather than centralized imposition.1,2,3,4,10,17This strategy acknowledges that AI is both necessary and hazardous, requiring robust tools and infrastructures but also strong ethical frameworks and community participation.3,4,10,17Institutional ambivalence is expressed in the coexistence of internal and external systems, in the emphasis on privacy‑by‑design and human‑centered principles, and in the commitment to open educational resources and communities of practice.3,4,15,17Feenberg’s concept of democratic technical politics suggests that institutions must remain open to revising their technological choices in light of public debate and evolving values.5Jonas’s imperative of responsibility demands that universities consider the long‑term consequences of AI, including its environmental impact, its role in shaping employment and social structures, and its effects on academic freedom and integrity.13Freirean critical education calls for institutions to foster learning environments where students and faculty can collectively interrogate AI and develop emancipatory practices.19,17By building infrastructures like CAIL and YorkGPT, articulating policies that restrict the use of confidential data in public AI tools, and investing in critical literacy initiatives, CUNY demonstrates an institutional commitment to ambivalence as method: AI is embraced cautiously, experimented with publicly, and subjected to ongoing scrutiny.2,3,4,10,17,19## Conclusion: Ambivalence, Responsibility, and Public AI Futures

The history and philosophy of technology, especially in the work of Andrew Feenberg and Hans Jonas, teaches that technology is an ambivalent dimension of social life, at once enabling and constraining, emancipatory and risky, and always shaped by political forces and ethical choices.5,13In the context of AI in higher education, this ambivalence is not something to be overcome by either enthusiastic adoption or categorical rejection; rather, it is a condition that must be recognized and mobilized for democratic, responsible practice.5,13,19The CUNY AI Lab and related initiatives—CUNY’s AI Academic Hub, the AI Innovation Fund, York College’s AI Vision and YorkGPT, and the Critical AI Literacy Institute—offer a concrete example of how a large public university system can institutionalize ambivalence as method.1,2,3,4,10,17By committing to open‑weight models, model‑agnostic infrastructures, and a public Model Registry, CAIL enables users to evaluate and compare AI systems rather than passively accept a single platform.2,4,11,12Its privacy‑by‑design architecture, including zero data retention in the Sandbox and attention to environmental costs, responds to Jonas’s demand for responsibility in the face of expanded technological power.4,13,15Faculty‑ and staff‑led governance, collaborative development of tools and documentation, and invitations to community participation embody Feenberg’s democratic technical politics, ensuring that AI remains open to public intervention and critique.2,5York College’s internal AI system, YorkGPT, and its guidance on data classification and academic integrity further demonstrate that institutions can build local infrastructures that align with their values while cautiously engaging with enterprise frameworks like Copilot.10,5,15Critical AI literacy initiatives such as CALI, grounded in Freirean critical education, cultivate subjects and learners who can inhabit ambivalence reflectively, using AI as an object of inquiry and a tool in collective knowledge‑making rather than as a substitute for intellectual labor.17,19Through seminars, institutes, discipline‑specific curricula, and open educational resources, CALI turns AI into a topic for democratic conversation across disciplines.17,19It thereby reinforces the Lab’s guiding principle of criticality, ensuring that AI literacy, judgment, and ethical awareness take precedence over dependency.4,17,19Together, these infrastructures and pedagogies demonstrate that public universities can design AI practices that keep questions open, allow for contestation, and sustain the possibility of alternative futures.1,2,3,4,10,17,19Contrasted with monolithic enterprise frameworks that centralize control, standardize data practices, and risk eroding critical literacy, CUNY’s approach highlights the importance of maintaining institutional and epistemic space for ambivalence.5,10,19Ambivalence here is not indecision but a principled refusal to collapse technology’s contradictions into simple narratives of progress or decline.5,13,19It is the stance that makes democratic technical politics possible, that recognizes the dual potentials of human freedom, and that insists on the intertwined character of technological power and ethical responsibility.5,13As AI continues to shape education, research, and administration, institutions that cultivate ambivalence through model‑agnosticism, privacy by design, shared governance, and critical literacy will be better positioned to steer technological change toward public, democratic ends.2,4,10,15,17,19
