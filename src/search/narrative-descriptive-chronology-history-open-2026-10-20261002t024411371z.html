Open-Source Innovation in Educational Technology at CUNY: From the Academic Commons to Manifold and the CUNY AI Lab

The City University of New York (CUNY) has spent nearly two decades building an interconnected ecosystem of open-source platforms that support teaching, research, and scholarly communication, beginning with the CUNY Academic Commons, extending through Commons In A Box and OpenLab, and evolving into the Manifold digital publishing platform and the recent work of the CUNY AI Lab.4,5,11,18,19,20,1,2,9,15Across this trajectory, CUNY has consistently treated digital infrastructure as an academic and civic project rather than a purely technical service, foregrounding openness, faculty governance, and critical engagement with technology.4,5,18,20This narrative and descriptive chronology traces how the university’s early commitment to open-source social networking for scholarship laid the groundwork for reusable platform toolkits, collaborative publishing environments, large-scale open educational resource (OER) initiatives, and, most recently, privacy-preserving and critically informed AI infrastructure.4,5,6,7,14,1,2,15In doing so, it highlights how CUNY’s distinctive institutional context—as the nation’s largest urban public university system—has shaped an approach to educational technology that aims to be democratic, sustainable, and deeply integrated into academic life.5,6,9## Framing Open-Source Educational Technology at CUNY

Conceptual and Institutional Background

The City University of New York serves a sprawling system of 25–26 colleges and graduate institutions, collectively described by early commentators as a “loose federation” characterized by significant fragmentation across campuses.5,9In the late 2000s, observers noted that static departmental websites and campus-specific systems did little to promote cross-campus collaboration or shared scholarly communities, contributing to a sense of institutional silos in both teaching and research.5Within this context, CUNY’s emerging digital initiatives were driven by faculty and technologists who saw the need for university-wide platforms that could transcend these silos, fostering communication and collaboration among faculty, graduate students, and undergraduates.5,4From the outset, CUNY’s approach to educational technology distinguished itself by prioritizing open-source software, community governance, and flexibility rather than relying on proprietary learning management systems.4,5,13,16This orientation reflected both ideological commitments to openness and practical concerns about cost, adaptability, and alignment with scholarly norms.4,5,6As a public university system serving a highly diverse student body, CUNY also faced a strong imperative to ensure that technology initiatives supported equitable access and could be shared or replicated beyond a single campus.6,18These priorities created fertile ground for a sequence of projects that would redefine how CUNY conceived of platforms for teaching, learning, publishing, and, later, AI-supported academic work.4,5,7,19,1,2The narrative that follows therefore treats open-source educational technology at CUNY not as an isolated series of tools, but as a long-term institutional strategy that responds to infrastructural, pedagogical, and scholarly challenges.4,5,6Each major project—the CUNY Academic Commons, Commons In A Box and OpenLab, Manifold, the OER Initiative’s infrastructure, and the CUNY AI Lab—emerged from concrete needs yet also fed into broader transformations in how CUNY understands digital scholarship and academic technology governance.5,18,19,20,1,2By reconstructing this chronology, we can see how CUNY moved from experimenting with a single social network for scholars to sustaining a multi-platform ecosystem that now encompasses open educational resources, networked publishing, and critically framed AI systems.4,5,6,7,14,1,2,15## The Birth of the CUNY Academic Commons

Origins, Vision, and Early Development

The CUNY Academic Commons originated in discussions among CUNY faculty and technologists in the late 2000s who identified a lack of university-wide digital spaces that could support cross-campus scholarly interaction.5At that time, CUNY’s colleges often maintained separate web presences, and there was no dedicated system-wide platform for collaborative academic work beyond email and local course management systems.5Faculty and staff concerned with digital pedagogy and scholarship argued that this fragmentation created “institutional silos,” limiting the circulation of teaching resources, research projects, and intellectual community among campuses.5In response to these concerns, the CUNY Committee on Academic Technology produced a 2008 white paper outlining a vision for an open-source, faculty-driven platform that could serve as an academic social network for the entire system.5This document emphasized that the platform should not simply replicate existing course management tools, but instead provide a flexible, user-controlled environment for sharing scholarship, building collaborative projects, and fostering interdisciplinary communication.5Crucially, the white paper treated the platform itself as an object of scholarly and technical experimentation, proposing that faculty and technologists collaboratively shape its development, rather than importing an off-the-shelf proprietary solution.5,4Following the white paper, the project moved quickly from conceptualization to implementation, launching a beta version of what became the CUNY Academic Commons in early 2009 and opening the platform more broadly by the end of that year.5The Commons was designed as an online academic social network restricted initially to CUNY faculty and graduate students, providing profiles, groups, and sites as the primary structural units for participation.5,4Its creators aimed to enable everything from collaborative working groups and departmental websites to course-related blogs and experimental publishing projects, all within a unified, system-wide infrastructure.5,4From its inception, the Commons embraced open-source technology as a core principle, basing its architecture on the WordPress content management system and the BuddyPress social networking framework.12,18WordPress provided a flexible, widely adopted platform for building websites and blogs, while BuddyPress added social features such as user profiles, groups, and activity streams, allowing the Commons to function as a full-fledged social network.12,18By choosing these open-source tools, the Commons team ensured that the platform could be customized, extended, and shared with other institutions, aligning technical decisions with broader commitments to openness and reusability.12,18,4### Addressing Institutional Silos and Building Community

The early years of the CUNY Academic Commons were marked by efforts to address the problem of institutional silos by encouraging cross-campus participation and community-building.5Faculty from different colleges used the platform to form groups that cut across departmental and institutional boundaries, creating spaces for shared research interests, pedagogical initiatives, and professional development.5,4Graduate students similarly found the Commons useful as a venue for organizing working groups, showcasing their research, and encountering scholars beyond their home campuses.5Initially, membership in the Commons was limited to faculty, administration, staff, and graduate students, reflecting the project’s origin as a platform for scholarly collaboration and professional networking.5However, instructors quickly began to adapt the platform for teaching, using its sites and groups as alternatives to conventional learning management systems that offered more flexibility and public engagement.5,4This experimentation highlighted the pedagogical potential of the Commons and helped drive its growth across the university.5,4By the mid-2010s, the Commons had expanded its membership policies to include undergraduates, following a significant membership cleanup and policy shift in 2017.5Opening the platform to undergraduate students marked a new phase in its evolution, transforming it from a primarily faculty–graduate scholar network into a broader teaching and learning environment that could host courses, student groups, and undergraduate research projects.5This shift increased the scale and diversity of the Commons community, setting the stage for its role in supporting open educational resources and student-centered scholarship.5,6As of 2025, the CUNY Academic Commons had registered over 53,000 members and hosted more than 34,000 sites, 2,000 groups, and 3,000 courses, illustrating the platform’s substantial growth and institutional impact.5These numbers reflect not only the adoption of the Commons as a teaching tool but also its role as a hub for sharing pedagogical resources, scholarly projects, and collaborative initiatives across the 25 colleges of the CUNY system.5,6The platform’s expansion over time has incorporated more robust group and site functionality, supporting everything from faculty development programs to student-run journals, thereby deepening its integration into CUNY’s academic life.5,4### Commons In A Box: Externalizing the Model

The success of the CUNY Academic Commons attracted attention beyond CUNY and led to efforts to package its underlying framework for use by other institutions and organizations.5,18In 2011, the project received a $107,500 grant from the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation to create “Commons In A Box,” a distributable open-source web framework that encapsulated the Commons’ infrastructure.5,12,18The goal was to reduce the complexity of creating digital commons spaces by providing a preconfigured suite of community and collaboration tools built on WordPress and BuddyPress.12,18Commons In A Box (CBOX) emerged as a free, open-source software project that enables organizations around the world to create vibrant online community spaces modeled on the CUNY Academic Commons.12,18CBOX is released under a GPLv3 license from the Free Software Foundation, ensuring that the code can be shared, modified, and redistributed in keeping with open-source principles.18The project provides an infrastructure that encourages discussion, collaboration, and sharing, aligning technical design with the social and scholarly goals of digital commons.18CBOX offers two main packages: CBOX Classic, designed for communities of all kinds, and CBOX OpenLab, specifically tailored for teaching, learning, and collaboration.12,18,17CBOX Classic provides flexible social networking capabilities that organizations can use to create commons spaces for research, project collaboration, and knowledge repositories, making it suitable for a wide range of academic and non-academic communities.12,18CBOX OpenLab, by contrast, incorporates features and functionality developed for City Tech’s OpenLab and digital humanities practice to create open learning environments that can serve entire educational institutions.17,18By extracting the infrastructure of the CUNY Academic Commons into Commons In A Box, CUNY not only consolidated its own technical investments but also contributed to a broader ecosystem of open-source educational platforms.12,18CBOX’s design encourages other institutions to adopt and adapt the commons model, illustrating how innovations at CUNY could ripple outward into the higher education landscape.18,19This externalization also reinforced CUNY’s identity as a hub for digital humanities and open-source educational technology, a role further solidified by subsequent projects such as Manifold.19,20## From Campus Platform to Open Pedagogy Hub: City Tech’s OpenLab and CBOX OpenLab

City Tech’s OpenLab as an Institutional Innovation

New York City College of Technology (City Tech), one of CUNY’s senior colleges, launched its own open-source digital platform, City Tech’s OpenLab, in Fall 2011.13,16,17The OpenLab was conceived as an open-source digital space where students, faculty, and staff could meet to learn, work, and share their ideas, with goals that included supporting teaching and learning, enabling connection and collaboration, and strengthening the intellectual and social life of the college community.13,16The project responded to the particular needs of a commuter campus, where physical dispersion made it challenging to sustain community and continuity beyond classroom encounters.13Technically, City Tech’s OpenLab is built using the same open-source foundations that undergird the CUNY Academic Commons: WordPress for content management and BuddyPress for social networking.13,16,17By relying on these widely adopted open-source platforms, the OpenLab team ensured that the skills students, faculty, and staff develop on the platform—including web publishing and basic site administration—would be transferable beyond the institution.13,16The OpenLab’s open-source architecture also allowed the team to customize features to support pedagogical experimentation and to contribute back to Commons In A Box through shared code and design.11,12,17,18Since its launch, City Tech’s OpenLab has grown into a community of more than 20,000 members, demonstrating its success in fostering openness and experimentation.13,16The platform has supported a wide variety of uses, ranging from course sites and student portfolios to departmental initiatives, faculty interest groups, and student clubs.13,17By encouraging public-facing work and collaboration across roles and departments, the OpenLab has helped City Tech integrate digital practices into everyday teaching and learning, while also serving as a demonstration site for the potential of open-source platforms in higher education.13,16,17### CBOX OpenLab: Generalizing OpenLab for Teaching and Learning

The innovative combination of WordPress, BuddyPress, and pedagogically oriented design in City Tech’s OpenLab became the basis for CBOX OpenLab, a Commons In A Box package specifically designed for teaching, learning, and collaboration.11,17,18CBOX OpenLab is a free open-source WordPress plugin that allows educational institutions to create commons spaces for open learning, modeled on the functionality of City Tech’s OpenLab.17,18It incorporates software developed by digital humanities practitioners and is intended to provide a powerful and flexible open alternative to costly proprietary educational platforms.17,18CBOX OpenLab was originally created through a collaboration between The Graduate Center, CUNY and City Tech, with funding from the National Endowment for the Humanities’ Office of Digital Humanities.18This NEH-supported effort built on earlier grants for Commons In A Box and explicitly linked open-source educational infrastructure to the broader field of digital humanities.18,5Ongoing feature development for CBOX OpenLab involves a multi-institution collaboration among the OpenLab teams at City Tech, Borough of Manhattan Community College, and SUNY Oneonta, illustrating the distributed and cooperative nature of its maintenance and innovation.18Designed to support collaboration and communication among students, faculty, and staff at any scale, CBOX OpenLab can be used for purposes ranging from individual course sites to institution-wide communities.17It facilitates teaching and learning by enabling open course spaces, but it also supports building open educational resources, managing departmental and college-wide initiatives, coordinating student internships, and creating professional portfolios.17As demonstrated by City Tech’s OpenLab, the platform’s flexibility allows it to be adapted to diverse institutional needs while maintaining a consistent core of open-source tools and collaborative structures.13,17,18By packaging the OpenLab model for broader use, CBOX OpenLab extends CUNY’s influence in the domain of open educational technology and allows other institutions to adopt an open, commons-based alternative to proprietary learning management systems.17,18This initiative situates CUNY as both a user and a producer of educational technology, contributing not only to its own internal infrastructure but also to the global ecosystem of open-source academic platforms.18,19The interplay between the Academic Commons, Commons In A Box, and OpenLab therefore demonstrates a cumulative approach to innovation: local platforms inform portable toolkits, which in turn stimulate new institutional experiments within and beyond CUNY.5,12,18## Manifold and the Transformation of Digital Publishing

Origins in Digital Humanities Collaboration

While the CUNY Academic Commons and OpenLab focused primarily on social networking, teaching, and collaborative community-building, Manifold emerged as a response to the limitations of traditional scholarly publishing in the digital age.19,20Manifold is an open-source, web-based digital publishing platform created through a collaboration between the CUNY Graduate Center, the University of Minnesota Press, and the digital development agency Cast Iron Coding.19,20The project debuted publicly in March 2018 and was conceived as a “networked, iterative, media-rich, interactive, and sustainable” way to integrate the web into publishing, reading, and teaching workflows.19,20The seeds of Manifold can be traced to 2013, when the GC Digital Scholarship Lab developed a web platform for the book “Debates in the Digital Humanities,” a print text published in 2012 that was transformed into a hybrid print/digital publication.20This experiment demonstrated the potential for web-based platforms to extend and reshape scholarly monographs by accommodating annotations, updates, and multimedia materials.20Building on this prototype, the Manifold team sought to create a generalized platform that other publishers and scholars could use to produce similarly rich and collaborative texts.19,20With support from the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation, the Manifold project secured multiple rounds of funding, including a $789,000 grant to support its second phase.20These grants enabled the team to refine the platform’s architecture, enhance its features, and expand partnerships with academic presses and scholarly communities.19,20Each participating organization—CUNY’s Graduate Center, the University of Minnesota Press, and Cast Iron Coding—brought distinct expertise in scholarship, publishing, and technology, respectively, allowing Manifold to integrate disciplinary perspectives and technical sophistication in ways that aligned with the needs of academic users.19,20Manifold was designed to be entirely open source and free, reflecting a deliberate commitment to openness and reusability.19,20The platform is a mobile-native application compatible with modern devices, including smartphones, tablets, notebooks, and desktops, ensuring that readers can access texts flexibly across contexts.19Through its open-source codebase, Manifold invites adaptations and extensions, while its documentation and governance align with the broader ethos of digital humanities, in which tools are treated as scholarly artifacts and sites of experimentation.19,20,7### Features and Scholarly Workflows

Manifold’s core functionality revolves around converting existing content—such as ebook files, word processing documents, or markdown texts—into responsive online publications that can be annotated, enriched with media, and iteratively updated.7,19,20Readers can annotate texts directly in the Manifold reader, highlight passages, and reply to others’ annotations, facilitating dialogue around scholarly works that would otherwise remain static.7,19Publishers can quickly turn existing ebook files into Manifold projects, reducing barriers to digital experimentation and making the platform suitable for both media-enriched and text-only projects.19,20A key conceptual innovation in Manifold is its embrace of iterative publishing, allowing authors and publishers to reveal a book’s evolution by releasing early drafts, gathering community feedback, and integrating annotations over time.20This functionality supports new modes of scholarly engagement, in which texts are not bound by the finality of print but instead become living documents that can incorporate ongoing research, teaching insights, and reader contributions.20,19Manifold’s architecture thus aligns closely with digital humanities practices that treat texts, tools, and scholarly communities as intertwined.19,20Within CUNY, Manifold is used for a diverse set of purposes that extend beyond conventional monographs.7,14,8,10Faculty use the platform to publish open course materials, work-in-progress scholarship, and digital monographs tailored to classroom use, often incorporating multimedia elements such as images, audio, video, and interactive data alongside text.7,14,19,20Students use Manifold to publish course-related writing and creative projects, transforming classroom outputs into public-facing scholarship and fostering collective authorship.7,8,10Scholarly collectives use the platform to produce journals and archival editions, leveraging its annotation features to facilitate scholarly dialogue.7,8,19Manifold’s attention to accessibility has also shaped its development and adoption.7The platform tracks progress on individual accessibility issues and maintains a public GitHub repository and roadmap of its efforts, enabling users to monitor and contribute to its accessibility improvements.7This focus is particularly important within CUNY, where ensuring that digital resources are accessible to a diverse student body is both a legal and ethical imperative.6,7By making accessibility a central design concern, Manifold exemplifies how open-source educational technology can align technical innovation with inclusion.7,19### The CUNY Manifold Instance and OER Publishing

CUNY has its own installation of Manifold, known as Manifold @CUNY, which serves as a free digital publishing platform for the entire university community.7,14,8,10This instance allows faculty, staff, and students to create and share their own scholarship, build custom classroom versions of texts and textbooks that are openly licensed or in the public domain, and publish open educational resources, journals, and other digital projects.7,14Through Manifold Reading Groups, instructors can assemble course readers from materials already available on the platform, enabling collaborative reading and annotation within classes.7,14The CUNY Manifold instance hosts hundreds of projects across disciplines, including original scholarship undergoing peer review, teaching handbooks, collaborative student projects, and customized course versions of public domain texts.8,10,14Featured collections on the platform showcase works such as “Barefoot in the Bronx,” student-produced memoirs, and “The Making of Race,” a course-related project on race and racism in the United States, illustrating how Manifold can support both research and pedagogy.8,10,14Archive projects include editions like “Adrienne Rich: Teaching at CUNY, 1968–1974,” produced through the Center for the Humanities, which highlight Manifold’s capacity to host historical and archival scholarship.8,20CUNY’s use of Manifold is closely tied to its broader commitment to OER publishing and digital humanities scholarship.6,19,20Manifold allows faculty to build open textbooks, annotated student editions, and course readers that are freely available to students and the public, thereby reducing textbook costs and expanding access to disciplinary materials.6,7,14For example, Graduate Center students have used Manifold to prepare public domain texts for classroom use, while faculty at other CUNY colleges have created OER textbooks on topics such as African-American literature.20These projects illustrate how Manifold can serve both as a scholarly publishing platform and as an engine for open education.6,7,19,20Within the CUNY OER Initiative, Manifold is recognized as a key piece of infrastructure for open educational practices.6In the 2024–2025 academic year, use of Manifold for OER and open educational practices continued to grow, with more than 200 new projects created and over 600 texts and 1,100 resources added to the platform.6Manifold Reading Group usage similarly expanded, with 52 new groups and nearly 12,000 annotations and comments added to Manifold texts, demonstrating the platform’s role in fostering interactive and collaborative learning.6,7The Manifold team supports the CUNY community through workshops, community events, campus visits, and curated project collections, helping instructors and students integrate the platform into teaching and scholarship.6,7,14By situating Manifold within an ecosystem that includes the Academic Commons, OpenLab, and Commons In A Box, CUNY has created a layered set of open-source tools that address different dimensions of academic work: social networking and course sites, institutional and community commons, and networked publishing and reading.4,5,11,13,17,18,7,14,19,20Together, these platforms enable teachers and scholars to move fluidly from collaboration to publication, with open-source infrastructure underlying each stage.18,19,20## Consolidating an Open Educational Ecosystem: The CUNY OER Initiative and Digital Toolkit

The OER Initiative as Infrastructure Strategy

CUNY’s open-source educational platforms have increasingly been integrated into a coordinated strategy around open educational resources, formalized through the CUNY OER Initiative.6This initiative supports a range of tools for CUNY scholars creating and publishing OER, aiming to make scholarship, teaching, and other work more openly available than is possible with proprietary platforms.6The OER Initiative recognizes that open-source technology and open licensing are intertwined, and that platforms like the Academic Commons, Manifold, and OpenLab can serve as infrastructures for producing and distributing OER.6,4,7,13In the 2024–2025 academic year, the OER Initiative documented extensive use of CUNY’s open platforms for open education efforts.6On the CUNY Academic Commons, faculty offered over 350 courses, and members continued to build out robust projects and websites that often functioned as OER.6,5The Commons Community Team organized workshops, campus visits, conference presentations, and open community meetings to support the platform’s more than 50,000 members.6,5The Commons Development team refined the technical infrastructure of the network, migrating the platform to an external server to achieve more reliable uptime and improved hosting, indicating ongoing investment in its stability and scalability.6,5The OER Initiative’s infrastructure portfolio extends beyond the Commons and Manifold to include platforms such as the OpenLab, WeBWorK, OpenEd CUNY, and Pressbooks.6,11,13,16City Tech’s OpenLab plays a critical role in supporting OER at that campus, hosting more than 160 OER projects built and maintained by faculty participants.6,13,16The OpenLab team partners with the OER program to develop and enhance functionality for OER creation and adoption, including a Resources directory that makes OER directly accessible from the site’s main navigation.6,13This integration demonstrates how open-source platforms can be tailored to foreground open educational content, making it easier for students and instructors to find and use OER.6,13WeBWorK, another platform supported by the OER Initiative, is an open-source online homework system for STEM courses that provides a zero-textbook-cost replacement for expensive proprietary platforms such as WebAssign.6Maintained in 2024–2025 by staff including Dragomir Saric and Blendi Koroveshi, WeBWorK saves students an estimated $185 in fees by replacing commercial homework systems.6OpenEd CUNY serves as a curated digital library of OER, enabling users to index pre-existing OER, build their own, and browse a CUNY OER Hub featuring resources created by and for CUNY.6Pressbooks, a dynamic web-based authoring tool, allows users to create, adapt, and adopt openly licensed content, including interactive materials produced through H5P and annotations via Hypothes.is, both integrated into the platform.6By coordinating these tools, the OER Initiative positions CUNY’s open-source platforms as a comprehensive infrastructure for open education, spanning content creation, distribution, and interactive engagement.6,7,13,16This ecosystem enables scholarly communities to choose the platform that best fits their pedagogical and disciplinary needs while ensuring that all tools operate within an overarching commitment to openness and public access.6,4,7,11,18It also reinforces CUNY’s ability to respond to economic pressures by reducing textbook costs, aligning educational technology with student affordability and equity.6,9### The Digital Toolkit and Cross-Platform Practices

To support faculty, staff, and students in navigating this expanding ecosystem, CUNY has developed a Digital Toolkit that includes guides for platforms such as the Academic Commons and Manifold.4,7The Digital Toolkit describes the Commons as an open-source platform designed to support academic community-building, project development and publishing, teaching and learning, and social networking across CUNY.4,5It frames the Commons as a central hub for sharing scholarship and connecting members of the university system, reinforcing its role in CUNY’s digital infrastructure.4,5,6Similarly, the Digital Toolkit presents Manifold as an open-source publishing platform built by scholars and publishers, highlighting its responsive, accessible, intuitive, and customizable design.7,19,20It emphasizes Manifold’s use by faculty, students, and scholarly collectives for publishing monographs, work-in-progress, open course materials, journals, and archival projects, while also noting features such as annotation, rich media integration, and mobile-responsive design.7,14,19,20These descriptions situate Manifold within everyday academic workflows, making it easier for potential users to understand how the platform might fit their teaching or research needs.7,6By providing centralized documentation and guidance, the Digital Toolkit helps faculty and students move across platforms, whether they are building a course site on the Commons, publishing a textbook on Manifold, or experimenting with OER on the OpenLab.4,6,7,13It also reinforces an understanding of educational technology as something to be learned and mastered, rather than passively consumed, aligning with the open-source ethos that users can become co-creators.4,13,16This pedagogical dimension of infrastructure is important in an institution like CUNY, where students benefit from acquiring transferable digital skills in publishing, collaboration, and open scholarship.13,16,7Taken together, the OER Initiative and the Digital Toolkit mark a phase in CUNY’s history where open-source platforms have moved from isolated innovations to a coordinated, institution-wide ecosystem.6,4,5,7,13This consolidation has allowed the university to make strategic investments in infrastructure, align platforms with curricular goals, and articulate a comprehensive vision of digital education that now extends into emerging AI initiatives.6,1,2,9## From Open Platforms to AI Infrastructure: The CUNY AI Lab

Founding Principles and Relationship to Prior Initiatives

The CUNY AI Lab, based at the CUNY Graduate Center, represents the latest stage in CUNY’s open-source educational technology trajectory, extending its values of openness, critical literacy, and academic governance into the domain of artificial intelligence.1,2,3,9,15The AI Lab is a faculty- and staff-led initiative that develops and maintains AI tools for teaching, learning, and research across CUNY.1,2,9It is part of the broader AI Academic Hub, whose mission is to harness AI’s potential to empower learning, drive research, and foster innovation while ensuring equitable opportunities for all within the public university system.3,9Like earlier infrastructural projects, the CUNY AI Lab prioritizes privacy, transparency, and thoughtful integration of technology into academic work, explicitly framing its tools as supports for scholarly and pedagogical goals rather than as opaque, externally imposed systems.1,2The AI Lab builds and maintains AI infrastructure for teaching, research, and academic work across CUNY, emphasizing zero-retention environments and curated open-weight models that align with institutional concerns over data governance and student privacy.1,2,9This orientation resonates with the values embedded in the Academic Commons, OpenLab, and Manifold, where open-source architecture and faculty leadership have been central.4,5,13,18,19,20The AI Lab draws on recent work such as the Google.org-sponsored Critical AI Literacy Institute (CALI), a program for CUNY faculty and students that examines how generative AI shapes knowledge production through workshop series, open teaching resources, and cross-disciplinary collaborations.15CALI reflects a commitment to “critical AI literacy,” which treats AI as a subject of analysis and critique as much as a tool, and the AI Lab’s work is guided by this principle alongside privacy, sustainability, and transparency.1,15In this sense, the AI Lab extends the critical digital humanities orientation that underpinned projects like Manifold and Commons In A Box into the AI domain.18,19,20,15### The CAIL Sandbox, Tools Portal, and Studios

At the core of the AI Lab’s infrastructure is the CAIL Sandbox, a zero-retention chat environment through which CUNY users can engage with curated open-weight models.1,2,9In the Sandbox, users can experiment with different language models, configure tools for research and classroom use, and prototype custom models for teaching, research, and critique.1,2The Sandbox’s zero-retention policy ensures that user data are not stored, addressing privacy concerns that frequently arise when universities adopt commercial AI platforms.1,2,9By focusing on open-weight models, the AI Lab also aligns with the open-source tradition, favoring systems whose architectures and training regimes are more transparent and modifiable than proprietary alternatives.1,2,15Alongside the Sandbox, the AI Lab runs a Tools Portal that supports research workflows, academic publishing, and direct model access.1,2Through this portal, users can access tools like Agent Studio, which supports research involving sources, data, files, drafts, and visualizations, and Site Studio, which undergirds portfolios, project sites, and other academic web publishing.1,2These studios extend earlier platform paradigms—such as the project sites on the Academic Commons and course spaces on the OpenLab—into environments where AI is embedded in tasks like summarization, data analysis, and text generation.4,13,16,1,2The AI Lab also provides media tools for transcription, image description, and optical character recognition (OCR), helping to make PDFs accessible and supporting multimodal scholarship and pedagogy.2These tools enable faculty and students to transform audio and visual materials into searchable, machine-readable formats, thereby enhancing accessibility and research capacity.2,6,7The Lab further assists with drafting AI use disclosure statements, recognizing that governance and transparency are critical in integrating AI into academic work.2,15By positioning itself as both a technical and ethical resource, the AI Lab echoes the dual focus on infrastructure and critical practice found in earlier digital humanities initiatives.18,19,20,15### AI Initiatives Across CUNY and the Academic Hub

The AI Lab operates within a broader network of AI-related initiatives at CUNY, coordinated in part through the AI Academic Hub.3,9Across the university’s 26 campuses, faculty, staff, and students lead hundreds of AI-related projects, including campus-level AI task forces, capacity-building incubators, AI literacy and workforce development programs, industry partnerships, and applied research.9Baruch College’s AI Think Tank, for example, is described as a federation of faculty and staff advancing excellence in AI in research, education, and operations, including the development of curricular frameworks and learning opportunities for students to build AI knowledge and skills.9Within this ecosystem, the CUNY AI Lab is identified as a faculty- and staff-led incubator for experimentation with AI.9The Lab team develops tools to address teaching and research needs, such as language partners for conversational practice, multilingual transcription systems, library assistants for automating cataloging and citation lookups, and purpose-built chatbots tailored to syllabi, readings, and course materials.9,1,2These applications demonstrate how AI can be integrated into everyday academic tasks while remaining under institutional control, rather than outsourced to commercial platforms.9,1,2By explicitly situating AI within an academic hub that emphasizes equitable opportunities and critical literacy, CUNY continues the tradition of treating technology as an object of governance and reflection.3,9,15The AI Lab’s infrastructure builds on the patterns established by the Academic Commons, OpenLab, and Manifold: faculty- and staff-led development, open-source or open-weight codebases, attention to accessibility, and integration into teaching and research workflows.4,5,13,18,19,20,1,2In doing so, it suggests that the university’s approach to AI is not a departure from its earlier digital projects but rather an extension of their underlying commitments into a new technological domain.1,2,15## Trajectories and Futures of Open-Source Innovation at CUNY

Continuities in Values, Governance, and Design

Across the history traced here—from the CUNY Academic Commons through Commons In A Box and OpenLab to Manifold and the CUNY AI Lab—certain values and design principles recur with striking consistency.4,5,11,13,18,19,20,1,2,6,7Foremost among these is a commitment to openness, understood not only in terms of open-source code but also as openness in community participation, knowledge sharing, and public access to educational resources.4,5,6,7,18,19The Commons, OpenLab, CBOX, and Manifold all foreground open-source foundations and GPL or similar licensing, allowing their codebases and configurations to be examined, modified, and redistributed.12,13,16,18,19The OER Initiative and Manifold’s use as a platform for open textbooks and student editions extend this commitment into content and pedagogy.6,7,14,20A second continuity lies in governance, where faculty and staff leadership has been central to each platform’s development and direction.4,5,13,18,19,20,1,2The Academic Commons was conceived and built by CUNY faculty and technologists, and its governance structures emphasize faculty-driven decision-making.5Commons In A Box and OpenLab similarly involve faculty and digital humanities practitioners in their design and maintenance.11,17,18Manifold emerged from collaboration between scholars, publishers, and technologists, with the GC Digital Scholarship Lab playing a major role.19,20The CUNY AI Lab is explicitly described as faculty- and staff-led, reinforcing this pattern.1,2,9This governance model ensures that platforms remain responsive to academic needs and critical perspectives, rather than being dictated by external vendors.4,18,19,1,2Third, CUNY’s digital projects consistently foreground accessibility and equity, especially in relation to the university’s public mission.6,7,13,16,19,20,1,2The OER Initiative explicitly aims to reduce textbook costs and expand access to course materials through zero-textbook-cost solutions like WeBWorK and open publications on Manifold and the Commons.6Manifold tracks accessibility issues and maintains public documentation and roadmaps, emphasizing inclusive design.7The AI Lab develops media tools for transcription and OCR to make PDFs and other materials accessible, while also prioritizing privacy and zero-retention environments.2,1These choices reflect a deep alignment between technical infrastructure and institutional commitments to serving a diverse, often economically constrained student population.6,9Finally, CUNY’s platforms embody a pedagogy of infrastructure, in which learning how to use and shape tools is part of the educational process.4,7,13,16,20,1,2The Academic Commons encourages faculty and students to create sites, groups, and projects that reflect their scholarly identities.5The OpenLab teaches students, faculty, and staff WordPress-based publishing skills that are transferable to broader contexts, framing technical literacy as a valuable outcome.13,16Manifold invites scholars and students to think about how texts can be iteratively published and annotated, integrating tool use into digital humanities methods.7,19,20The AI Lab’s CAIL Sandbox offers an environment where users can experiment with models and configurations, supported by critical AI literacy programs.1,2,15In each case, the platform is not only a service but also a site of learning and critical reflection.4,13,19,15### Emerging Directions and Challenges

Looking forward, the integration of AI into CUNY’s open-source ecosystem raises both opportunities and challenges.1,2,9,15The AI Lab’s focus on open-weight models, privacy, and critical literacy suggests an approach that resists the commodification and opacity often associated with commercial AI systems.1,2,15At the same time, the rapid evolution of AI technologies demands ongoing vigilance to ensure that tools remain aligned with academic values and that faculty, staff, and students are equipped to understand and critique their implications for knowledge production.15,9There are also opportunities to deepen the connections between platforms like Manifold and Commons and AI-supported tools, such as intelligent annotation assistants, multilingual reading environments, or automated metadata generation for scholarly projects.7,19,20,1,2The challenge lies in integrating such tools in ways that enhance scholarly and pedagogical practices without undermining privacy, autonomy, or the interpretive labor that is central to humanities and social science scholarship.15,19,20The AI Lab’s emphasis on configuration and zero-retention environments provides a foundation for such careful integration.1,2Within the OER landscape, AI might be used to support the creation, adaptation, and personalization of open resources, raising questions about authorship, authority, and the dynamics of collaboration between human and machine systems.6,7,1,2CUNY’s experience with open-source platforms and digital humanities collaboration positions it well to lead critical experiments in AI-supported open education, particularly if governance structures ensure that faculty and students remain central to decision-making.4,6,18,20,1,2The CALI program’s work on how generative AI shapes knowledge production exemplifies this emerging focus.15In sum, CUNY’s history of open-source innovation in educational technology—from the Academic Commons to OpenLab and CBOX, through Manifold, and into the AI Lab—provides a rich foundation for navigating the complex terrain of AI in higher education.4,5,11,13,18,19,20,1,2,6,7,9,15The continuity of values and governance across these projects suggests that even as technologies change, the university can continue to steer infrastructural development toward democratic, accessible, and critically informed ends.4,6,18,20,1,2,15## Conclusion

The narrative and descriptive chronology of open-source educational technology at CUNY reveals a sustained, institutionally grounded trajectory that begins with the CUNY Academic Commons and extends through Commons In A Box, City Tech’s OpenLab, the Manifold digital publishing platform, the OER Initiative’s infrastructure, and the CUNY AI Lab.4,5,11,13,18,19,20,6,7,1,2,9,15Each stage in this history responds to specific institutional challenges—whether the problem of campus silos, the need for flexible teaching platforms, the limitations of print publishing, the high cost of textbooks, or the ethical complexities of AI—yet builds on and reinforces a shared set of commitments to openness, accessibility, faculty governance, and critical literacy.4,5,6,7,18,19,20,1,2,15The Academic Commons addressed fragmentation by creating a university-wide, open-source academic social network, which later became the model for Commons In A Box, allowing other institutions to adopt similar infrastructures.5,12,18City Tech’s OpenLab demonstrated how a campus-specific platform could foster open pedagogy and community in a commuter college, and CBOX OpenLab generalized this model for broader use.13,16,17,18Manifold transformed digital publishing by offering an open-source, media-rich, annotated, and iterative environment for texts, which CUNY harnessed as a core tool for OER and digital humanities scholarship.7,14,19,20The OER Initiative and Digital Toolkit consolidated these platforms into a coordinated ecosystem that supports open course materials, STEM homework, digital libraries, and collaborative authoring.6,4,7,11,13,16Most recently, the CUNY AI Lab has extended these traditions into AI, building open-weight, privacy-preserving, and critically framed infrastructures for teaching, research, and academic workflows.1,2,9,15By anchoring AI initiatives within a faculty- and staff-led lab and a system-wide AI Academic Hub, CUNY continues to treat technology as a domain of governance and scholarship rather than merely service provision.3,9,15In doing so, it lays the groundwork for future integrations of AI into open-source educational platforms that can enhance learning and research while maintaining the university’s longstanding commitments to equity, openness, and critical engagement.4,6,18,20,1,2,15Taken together, this chronology underscores that open-source innovation at CUNY is not simply a set of tools but an evolving institutional practice—a way of thinking about and building educational technology that is deeply intertwined with the university’s public mission and scholarly cultures.4,5,6,7,18,19,20,1,2,9,15As AI and other emerging technologies continue to reshape higher education, CUNY’s history offers an important example of how a large public system can cultivate digital infrastructures that are participatory, sustainable, and oriented toward the collective production of knowledge.
