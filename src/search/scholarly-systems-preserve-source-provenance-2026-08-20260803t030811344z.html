Provenance, References, and Durable Archives for Scholarly AI Systems in Higher-Education Research Workflows

The rapid integration of generative artificial intelligence into higher-education research workflows has collided with long-standing scholarly expectations around verifiable sources, complete reference lists, and durable access to cited materials.6,9As AI tools increasingly participate in literature discovery, drafting, code generation, and data handling, they risk amplifying existing problems—such as reference rot and incomplete citation of data, software, and web resources—unless they are designed to preserve provenance and connect outputs to sustainable archival infrastructure.3,7,11,17At the same time, mature components of scholarly cyberinfrastructure now exist, including a stabilised layer of persistent identifiers for people, organisations, projects, and outputs, standardised models for provenance, and pragmatic packaging formats such as RO-Crate, which are explicitly aimed at making research FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) and citable.1,14,16,20This report argues that scholarly AI systems should be engineered as first-class citizens in this ecosystem: capturing detailed provenance of prompts and sources, generating complete and standards-compliant reference lists that encompass publications, data, and software, and automatically anchoring web and grey-literature citations in durable public archives like Perma.cc and Internet Archive Scholar.3,7,11,12,17By leveraging persistent identifiers, provenance ontologies, research object packaging, and emerging institutional policies for acknowledging generative AI, universities can align AI-assisted research practices with the long-term integrity of the scholarly record rather than working at cross-purposes to it.1,6,9,14,16,20## 1. Introduction: AI Systems in the Scholarly Communication Ecosystem

The last several years have seen generative AI tools move from experimental curiosities to everyday instruments in research-intensive universities, where they are used to brainstorm questions, summarise literature, draft sections of manuscripts, translate or edit text, and even generate or refactor code for analysis pipelines.6,8,9Academic libraries and institutions now routinely issue guidance stating that any use of generative AI in the preparation of scholarly work must be acknowledged, whether the tool was employed to generate content, edit text, assist with literature review, or manipulate data.6,9This guidance reflects a consensus that AI tools cannot be treated as invisible background utilities; rather, they create new artefacts and transformations that must be documented for reasons of transparency, accountability, and research integrity.6,9At the same time, these AI systems are being deployed into a scholarly environment whose information infrastructure has itself become more sophisticated and structured.1,5By 2026, a “PID quartet” has stabilised as the core persistent-identifier layer for scholarship: ORCID for individual researchers, ROR for organisations, RAiD for research projects and activities, and DOI for a wide range of research outputs including articles, datasets, and software.1,5Each of these identifiers is intended to provide a global, machine-resolvable reference to a specific entity or activity, creating a graph of relationships that can track who did what, at which institution, within which project, and with which outputs.1,5For scholarly AI systems, this PID graph represents not just background infrastructure but a critical substrate for recording provenance and generating structured, verifiable references.

Despite these advances, a longstanding fragility remains in the web-based portions of the scholarly record, where references to websites, datasets, and software often decay over time through link rot and content drift.3,17Studies under the Hiberlink project and related work have shown that a substantial fraction of web-based references in recent scholarly articles are already “rotten” within a few years of publication, meaning that the link either returns an error or resolves to content that no longer reflects the citing author’s original intent.17This phenomenon, termed reference rot, threatens the long-term usability and interpretability of research, particularly in areas that rely heavily on online resources, code repositories, or documentation.17AI systems that automatically discover, summarise, and cite web resources will encounter and potentially propagate this fragility unless they incorporate durable archiving mechanisms from the outset.3,12,17The challenge, then, is to design scholarly AI research systems that are tightly integrated into existing mechanisms for provenance, citation, and archiving, rather than functioning as isolated black boxes that emit context-free text with unverifiable references.6,9,20Provenance in this context needs to capture not only traditional relationships between datasets, software, and results, but also the provenance of AI-mediated transformations: which prompts were used, which sources were consulted, which model version generated which passage, and how those outputs were subsequently edited.6,9,20Reference management must evolve beyond listing articles to encompass data, code, and AI outputs themselves, using community-endorsed citation principles.4,7Public archival infrastructures such as Perma.cc and Internet Archive Scholar need to be invoked automatically wherever AI tools draw on or cite web-based materials.3,12,17This report proceeds by first outlining the foundational components that make provenance and durable citation technically feasible: persistent identifiers, provenance ontologies, and research object packaging formats.1,14,16,20It then examines how scholarly AI systems can capture and expose rich source provenance, discussing what needs to be recorded and how it might be represented in standard vocabularies.6,9,20Subsequently, it considers mechanisms for ensuring complete and standards-compliant reference lists, including data and software, and for properly acknowledging AI’s role in the writing and analysis process.4,6,7,9The report then turns to durable web archiving and the mitigation of reference rot, highlighting services such as Perma.cc and Internet Archive Scholar and their relevance for AI-assisted citation workflows.3,12,17Finally, it synthesises these elements into end-to-end workflow patterns suitable for higher-education institutions and reflects on governance and training implications.6,9,11,16## 2. Foundational Infrastructure: Persistent Identifiers, Provenance Models, and Research Object Packaging

2.1 The Persistent Identifier Layer for Scholarly Entities

Persistent identifiers are now widely recognised as a cornerstone of modern scholarly communication, providing stable, globally unique references to the key entities involved in research.1,5In the contemporary PID “quartet,” ORCID assigns a 16-digit persistent identifier to individual researchers, expressed as a URI such as \(https://orcid.org/0000-0002-1825-0097\), which remains associated with that person across name changes, institutional moves, and multiple publication venues.1,5These identifiers are compatible with the ISO 27729 International Standard Name Identifier format and are maintained by a nonprofit organisation that coordinates with publishers, repositories, and institutional CRIS systems.5For AI systems, ORCID enables the unambiguous linking of cited works and datasets to their human creators, supporting accurate attribution and disambiguation of similarly named authors in retrieved sources, which is critical for trustworthy citation generation.1,5Complementing ORCID at the organisational level is the Research Organization Registry (ROR), an open registry that assigns persistent ROR IDs to research institutions, funders, and other organisations.1,5ROR IDs were developed to solve a pervasive interoperability problem: disparate systems often record institutional affiliations in free text, with varying spellings, abbreviations, and name changes, making it difficult to aggregate outputs by institution.5By 2026, ROR has become the default organisational PID across major infrastructures such as Crossref, DataCite, ORCID, and institutional CRIS systems, meaning that author affiliations in publication metadata routinely resolve to ROR IDs that travel with the record.1AI research systems that harvest metadata via Crossref or similar APIs can thus associate authors and funders with specific organisations in a machine-actionable way, and they can propagate these identifiers into their own provenance records.1,5The Research Activity Identifier (RAiD) extends the PID paradigm from people and organisations to projects and research activities themselves.1,5RAiD, now standardised as ISO 23527:2022, is designed to identify a research project across its full lifecycle, traversing institutional boundaries and funding transitions where necessary.1,5A RAiD record can name the project, list participants by ORCID, associate institutions and funders via ROR or other registries, and enumerate outputs through DOIs and other PIDs.1,5Funder workflows increasingly issue or register a RAiD upon award, with project teams updating the record as activities unfold and outputs are produced.1For AI systems, RAiD provides a natural anchor when generating or consuming project-level documentation and provenance, enabling the system to tag its own outputs as belonging to a specific research activity whose participants and outputs are already catalogued.1,5At the output level, the Digital Object Identifier (DOI) remains the most widely used PID for journal articles, datasets, preprints, and increasingly for software, instruments, and samples.1,5DOIs are standardised by ISO 26324:2012 and are assigned by registration agencies such as Crossref and DataCite, which maintain resolution infrastructure so that each DOI points to a landing page describing the object, even if local URLs change over time.5For AI citation generation, resolving references to DOIs rather than bare URLs provides greater durability and metadata richness, including structured information about authors, titles, publication years, and related identifiers that can be integrated into reference lists and provenance graphs.1,5In a typical piece of research, all four PID types may intersect: a researcher (ORCID) at an institution (ROR) works on a project (RAiD) that produces outputs (DOIs), and a well-designed AI system should be able to both consume and emit this structure.1,5### 2.2 Provenance Ontologies and the Role of PROV-O

While persistent identifiers address the “what” and “who” of research objects, provenance models address the “how,” “when,” and “why” by describing the relationships among entities, activities, and agents.20The W3C PROV-O ontology provides a formal, OWL-based specification for representing provenance information in a machine-readable, interoperable way.20In PROV-O, an Entity represents some thing in the world—such as a dataset, a document, a software package, or an AI-generated summary—while an Activity represents something that occurs over a period of time and acts upon or with entities.20An Agent represents something that bears responsibility for an activity, such as a person, organisation, or software system.20These core classes are connected by properties such as `prov:wasGeneratedBy`, `prov:used`, and `prov:wasAttributedTo`, enabling detailed graphs that show how derived entities depend on antecedent ones.20PROV-O is structured into components, including agents-responsibility, derivations, entities-activities, collections, and annotations, each capturing different aspects of provenance in a modular fashion.20For example, derivation relationships can specify that one dataset was derived from another through a particular transformation, while agents-responsibility may indicate that a particular researcher or institution bears responsibility for an activity that used specific data and produced certain results.20Crucially, PROV-O also supports the concept of bundles, which are named sets of provenance descriptions that are themselves entities; this allows the provenance of provenance to be expressed, such as who asserted a given provenance record and when.20For AI research systems, this capacity is vital: the system’s own logs of prompts, model versions, and source retrievals can be packaged as a provenance bundle, with clear attribution to the AI service and the human user.20Mapping AI interactions onto PROV-O is conceptually straightforward and technically powerful.20An AI-generated literature review can be modeled as an entity `E_review` that `prov:wasGeneratedBy` an activity `A_inference`, which `prov:used` entities representing the retrieved source articles, datasets, and code repositories, each identified by DOIs or other PIDs.1,4,7,20The activity `A_inference` can be `prov:wasAssociatedWith` an agent representing the AI model (e.g., a specific version of a large language model) and another agent representing the human researcher who initiated the query.6,9,20The prompt itself can be modeled as an entity used by the inference activity, while subsequent human revisions to the AI-generated text can be represented as further activities that derive new entities from the original AI output.6,9,20By adopting PROV-O as a common provenance language, scholarly AI systems can expose their internal operations in a way that aligns with broader efforts to capture computational provenance in science and humanities research.14,16,20### 2.3 RO-Crate: Packaging Research Artefacts and Metadata for FAIR Reuse

Beyond identifiers and ontologies, there is a practical need for formats that bundle research artefacts and their metadata into self-describing packages that can be exchanged, archived, and reused.14,16RO-Crate is a lightweight, community-driven specification that addresses this need by defining conventions for packaging data, software, workflows, and other research materials along with rich metadata using JSON-LD and schema.org.14,16An RO-Crate is essentially a directory (or archive) containing research files plus a single JSON-LD file, `ro-crate-metadata.json`, located at the root, which describes the contents and their relationships in a machine-readable way.16This metadata file includes a Root Data Entity that represents the crate as a whole—typically typed as a schema.org `Dataset`—from which all other entities, such as files, people, organisations, and software, are linked via typed relationships.16RO-Crate emerged from the Research Object community’s earlier efforts to create semantically rich aggregations of distributed resources that could encapsulate an entire research study, including spreadsheets, code, workflows, and resulting figures.14The RO concept emphasises not only bundling diverse artefacts but also annotating them with relationships that express their context—for example, that a CSV file is used by a script, or that a figure results from a particular workflow execution.14RO-Crate applies “just enough” Linked Data standards to make these packages both human-understandable and machine-actionable, supporting the FAIR data principles by specifying creators, licenses, provenance, and structural relationships in a consistent, interoperable way.14,16It is used across domains such as bioinformatics, digital humanities, and regulatory science to facilitate reproducible and citable sharing of complex research outputs.14,16For AI research systems, RO-Crate offers a natural vehicle for exporting the results of an AI-assisted workflow in a manner that is immediately ready for archiving and reuse.14,16An AI tool that helps a researcher assemble a literature corpus, generate a draft systematic review, and run some exploratory analyses could package the final text, the intermediate notes, the list of retrieved articles (with DOIs), the code used for analysis, and a structured provenance log into a single RO-Crate.1,4,7,14,16The `ro-crate-metadata.json` file could incorporate PROV-O statements describing how each derived file relates to its inputs, as well as PIDs for creators (ORCID), institutions (ROR), projects (RAiD), and outputs (DOI).1,16,20This crate could then be deposited into an institutional repository or domain-specific archive, where it remains accessible, citable, and amenable to machine analysis for years to come.14,16### 2.4 Data and Software Citation as Precedent and Template

The challenges of citing data and software in a systematic, machine-actionable way predate generative AI and provide important precedents for designing AI-aware scholarly workflows.4,7The Software Citation Principles, articulated by Smith and colleagues under the Force11 umbrella, recommend that software citations minimally include the creator (authors or project), title (software name), and identifier (preferably a persistent, resolvable pointer such as a DOI to a landing page with metadata).4Additional recommended elements include the date of creation or publication, the specific version being referenced, and the publisher or repository hosting the software.4These principles aim to encourage consistent practice so that software used in research can be properly credited, discovered, and reused, much like articles.4Similarly, guidelines for citing research data emphasise that data should be treated as first-class scholarly outputs, with citations including creators, dataset title, year of publication, version, publisher (institution or repository), and a persistent identifier such as a DOI.7Some recommendations add explicit labeling of the resource type (e.g., “[Dataset]”) and suggest including analysis software, date accessed, or other contextual information where relevant.7For code, guidance often mirrors software citation principles, calling for creator, title, identifier, date, version, publisher, and resource-type tags such as “[Software]” or “[ComputationalNotebook]”.7These practices recognise that robust research transparency requires visibility not only into narrative articles but also into the underlying artefacts that make results reproducible.4,7Scholarly AI systems that assist with literature reviews, methods sections, or data management should therefore be designed to surface and construct data and software citations that adhere to these principles, rather than focusing solely on article-level references.4,7When an AI tool discovers and recommends a dataset from a repository, it should be able to generate a complete data citation with DOI, creator, title, and version, and to integrate this citation into the manuscript’s reference list.4,7When it generates or modifies code, it should support attribution to any external libraries or software it utilises, providing structured citations or links to code repositories in line with software citation norms.4,7In this sense, the existing consensus around data and software citation offers a template for extending citation practices to AI-produced content and for integrating AI into workflows where multiple research object types are routinely cited and archived.4,6,7,9## 3. Capturing and Preserving Source Provenance in Scholarly AI Systems

3.1 What Counts as Provenance in AI-Assisted Research?

Provenance in AI-assisted research must be conceptualised more broadly than in traditional workflows because the AI system itself becomes an active participant in data transformation and knowledge synthesis.6,9,20At a minimum, provenance for AI-generated content includes the user’s prompts or queries, the set of external sources the system consulted (including publications, datasets, and web pages), the model identity and version, the time and context of generation, and any subsequent edits applied by the human researcher.6,9,20Additionally, provenance may need to capture upstream computational pipelines, such as retrieval-augmented generation steps that perform keyword searches, ranking, and filtering of documents before passing them to the language model.20Each of these stages involves entities, activities, and agents that can be modeled using PROV-O, providing a detailed trace from inputs to outputs.20Institutional guidelines on generative AI use already implicitly recognise the need to track some of this provenance, even if they do not frame it in ontological terms.6,9For example, several universities recommend that if AI tools are used for drafting ideas, planning, structuring written materials, translating, or editing, the use should be acknowledged in an appendix or methods section, often including the name and version of the AI system, the prompts used, the date of output generation, and a description of how the outputs were edited or incorporated.6,9Such guidance frames provenance at the level of documentation, emphasising that researchers bear responsibility for verifying AI-generated content and must disclose functional uses of AI even when they do not directly quote or paraphrase AI outputs.6,9Scholarly AI systems can make compliance with these expectations far easier if they automatically log and package this information as part of their normal operation.6,9,20From a provenance perspective, AI systems themselves are both agents and activities: the model and its hosting platform can be modeled as an agent bearing responsibility for the generative activity, while each specific inference run is an activity that uses inputs and generates outputs, potentially under supervision by a human agent.20The sources retrieved and passed to the model, including articles, datasets, and code, are entities that can be linked to external PIDs such as DOIs, ORCID iDs, ROR IDs, and RAiDs.1,4,7,20AI systems should therefore be engineered to treat source retrieval and usage as first-class events in their internal architecture, producing explicit records of which entities were actually used in each generative step, rather than implicitly mixing many sources or opaque training data.1,6,9,20Even when training data cannot be exhaustively enumerated, the provenance of retrieval-augmented sources and citations can and should be captured with precision.1,20### 3.2 Representing AI Provenance with PROV-O and RO-Crate

Once an AI system captures provenance for its operations, the next question is how to represent and expose that provenance in an interoperable form.14,16,20PROV-O provides the abstract ontology for modelling the relationships, while RO-Crate offers a concrete packaging format that can embed PROV-O statements in a JSON-LD file alongside research artefacts.14,16,20A scholarly AI system might, for example, create an RO-Crate for each major session or project, with the Root Data Entity representing the session’s overall output (such as a draft manuscript or structured set of notes), and additional entities for each intermediate file, prompt, and retrieved source.14,16The `ro-crate-metadata.json` file can contain PROV-O statements indicating which entities were used by which activities and which agents were responsible.16,20In this representation, each AI inference activity would be an instance of `prov:Activity` that `prov:used` entities representing the prompt and the selected source documents, and `prov:generated` a new entity representing the AI’s textual output.16,20The AI model itself could be represented as an `prov:Agent`, possibly subclassed or annotated as software, with attributes recording its name, version, and provider.4,6,9,20The human researcher would be another agent, identified by ORCID and institutional affiliation linked via ROR.1,5,20If the project has a RAiD, that identifier can be associated with the Root Data Entity or a contextual project entity in the crate, providing a bridge to funder and institutional project registries.1,5,16This tight coupling of PROV-O semantics, PIDs, and RO-Crate packaging yields a machine-readable record of AI provenance that can travel alongside the research outputs into repositories, archives, or collaborative platforms.14,16,20RO-Crate’s use of JSON-LD and schema.org makes it accessible to a broad range of tools, while still allowing inclusion of discipline-specific or workflow-specific vocabularies.14,16For AI provenance, this flexibility means that, in addition to PROV-O, one could include terms that describe model hyperparameters, inference settings, or evaluation metrics, enriching the record beyond the minimum needed for traceability.14,16,20The Research Object tradition of bundling datasets, workflows, scripts, and figures together with annotations about their interrelations is especially apt for AI-intensive research where code, data, and narrative are all entangled.14By standardising how AI provenance is embedded in RO-Crates, communities can develop tooling for visualising, querying, and validating provenance, enabling auditors, reviewers, and future researchers to understand and assess the role of AI in the research process.14,16,20### 3.3 Institutional Expectations and Disclosure of AI Use

The representational strategies described above align with emerging institutional and style-guide expectations that AI use must be disclosed and documented.6,9,10Many universities instruct students and researchers that the use of generative AI as a functional tool in creating academic work—whether for idea generation, structuring, summarising, translation, or editing—must be acknowledged, typically in an appendix or methods section.6This acknowledgement is expected to include at least the name and version of the AI system, the publisher or provider, the URL of the tool, and a brief description of the context in which it was used.6,9Additional recommended details include the prompts employed, the date outputs were generated, examples of the AI outputs themselves, and a description of how those outputs were edited or transformed before incorporation into the work.6,9These elements map neatly onto the provenance data that an AI system could automatically record and export.6,9,20Furthermore, citation-style organisations and academic libraries increasingly provide guidance on when and how AI-generated content should be cited, and they consistently emphasise that AI cannot be an author and that human oversight and responsibility remain paramount.2,6,9,10Some styles recommend treating AI outputs as software or as a form of personal communication, often discouraging inclusion in the reference list unless a stable URL to the chat transcript is available, and instead favouring in-text notes or acknowledgements.2,6,9,10They also stress that users must verify any sources suggested by AI tools, since generative models can hallucinate citations or misrepresent referenced works.2,9Scholarly AI systems can support these expectations by clearly labelling AI-generated text, distinguishing between content directly quoted from sources and synthetic summarisation, and by linking every suggested reference to resolvable identifiers such as DOIs for verification.1,4,7,9By embedding provenance capture and disclosure formats into system design, AI providers can reduce the cognitive and administrative burden on researchers, making it easier for them to comply with institutional and style-guide requirements.6,9,11For example, an AI system could generate a concise methods-paragraph describing how the tool was used, including model name, version, provider, and a high-level summary of tasks performed, drawing directly on the logged provenance data.6,9,20It could also produce an appendix-ready export of prompts and responses, or a link to an archived transcript, for inclusion in supplementary materials.6,9,12Such features would move AI systems from being potential sources of opacity and misconduct to active enablers of good scholarly practice.6,9,11## 4. Ensuring Complete and Standards-Compliant Reference Lists in AI-Assisted Authoring

4.1 From Suggested Sources to Verified Citations

One of the most visible roles of AI tools in scholarly workflows is assisting with literature discovery and citation generation, yet this is also an area where generative models are prone to fabricating plausible but nonexistent references.2,9Academic guidance consistently warns users not to rely on AI-generated citations without independent verification, noting that AI tools may both invent references and misrepresent the content of real sources.2,6,9Thus, scholarly AI systems must be designed to treat reference suggestions as hypotheses rather than authoritative facts, providing mechanisms for users to inspect, verify, and correct references before they enter the final manuscript.6,9This implies that systems should, whenever possible, ground their references in actual retrieval of bibliographic records from trusted indexes, repositories, or archives, rather than constructing them purely from model-generated text.1,11,12One promising design pattern is to integrate AI generation with bibliographic infrastructures that operate on DOIs and other PIDs, such as Crossref, DataCite, and open-source manuscript platforms like Manubot.1,11Manubot, for instance, allows authors to cite works by providing identifiers such as DOIs, PubMed IDs, or URLs, and then automatically generates and numbers citations according to a chosen style, updating the manuscript as changes are made.11It can transform markdown manuscripts into HTML, PDF, or DOCX, track versions via git, and orchestrate continuous integration workflows for collaborative scientific writing.11An AI system integrated with such infrastructure could, upon suggesting a source, immediately resolve the DOI and display the canonical metadata, giving the user confidence that the reference corresponds to a real, accessible work.1,11It could also populate the manuscript’s citation keys and reference list in a format consistent with the author’s chosen style, while still requiring the human author to confirm inclusion.11Ensuring completeness of reference lists also requires that AI systems be aware of non-article sources, such as datasets and software, and apply appropriate citation templates.4,7When an AI tool identifies that a user’s analysis relies on a particular dataset or software package, it should prompt the user to include data and software citations that specify creators, titles, versions, publishers, and persistent identifiers.4,7Rather than leaving these citations as free-text, the system should construct them following established data and software citation guidelines, marking them explicitly as “[Dataset]” or “[Software]” where recommended.4,7This not only improves credit to data and software creators but also strengthens the reproducibility of the research, since future readers can locate the exact materials used.4,7AI systems can assist by querying repository APIs to fill in missing metadata and by flagging inconsistencies for human review.7,11### 4.2 Citing AI Tools Themselves

Another dimension of completeness in reference lists concerns whether and how to cite the AI tools themselves when they contribute to a scholarly work.2,6,8,9,10Many academic libraries advise that functional uses of AI—for example, using a model to edit, translate, or generate content—should be disclosed in the methods or acknowledgements, rather than treating the AI as a conventional author.6,8,9,10Some guidance suggests that, in cases where AI-generated content is substantively discussed or analysed, it may be appropriate to treat the output as a source with no human author, or to cite the AI tool as software, following software citation formats.4,6,9,10In such cases, reference entries may list the developer or organisation as the author, the name and version of the model as the title, a description such as “[Large language model]”, the date of the model or chat, and a URL to the tool or archived transcript.2,6,9,10However, style manuals differ in how they recommend handling AI in reference lists.2,6,9,10Some, like certain interpretations of Chicago style, prefer to treat AI outputs as personal communications cited in notes but not included in bibliographies, on the grounds that the conversation transcripts are private and not publicly retrievable.2,6,9,10Others, like some evolving APA and MLA guidelines, acknowledge that if a stable URL or shareable transcript is available, AI-generated content may be cited in the reference list, especially when it is directly quoted or central to the argument.2,6,9,10Across these variations, there is general agreement that human authors remain responsible for the content of their work and that AI tools should not be credited as authors in the conventional sense.2,6,9,10Scholarly AI systems need to be aware of these differences and may need to support multiple citation strategies depending on the style chosen by the user.2,6,9,10Designing AI systems to assist with AI-tool citation requires them to track sufficient provenance information about their own use to generate candidate references or acknowledgements.6,9,20As discussed earlier, this includes logging the model name and version, provider, date and time of usage, and a concise description of the prompts and tasks performed.6,9,20The system can then propose appropriate text for the acknowledgements or methods section, as well as a reference entry if the style demands it, drawing on software citation templates where relevant.4,6,7,9By doing so, AI systems help normalise transparent disclosure of their role and reduce the risk that authors will omit this information out of confusion or oversight.6,9They also implicitly reinforce the notion that AI outputs are sources to be critically evaluated and documented, not invisible assistants.6,9### 4.3 Human-in-the-Loop Verification and Responsibility

Even with strong provenance capture and automated citation support, human oversight remains essential, both ethically and practically.2,6,9Institutional guidelines emphasise that authors are responsible for verifying the accuracy and originality of AI-generated content and for ensuring that AI use does not involve fabrication or falsification of data.6,9They stress the necessity of human oversight at all stages of research and writing, from design through analysis to dissemination, and warn that over-reliance on AI without verification can constitute poor academic practice or even misconduct.6,9Therefore, AI systems should be designed to foreground this responsibility, making it clear when content is AI-generated, prompting users to verify references, and providing interfaces that facilitate checking rather than obscuring sources.6,9Practically, this implies that AI interfaces should display not only the text they generate but also structured side-panels or overlays showing the provenance and citations that underlie each passage.11,14,20For example, a paragraph summarising three articles could include interactive markers that reveal the DOIs, authors, and publication details of the underlying sources, alongside links to full texts where available, such as archived versions in Internet Archive Scholar.1,11,12The system could flag any references that could not be resolved to a DOI or trusted record, prompting the user to investigate or discard them.1,11,12For web-based sources, it could show both the live URL and a Perma.cc archived link, along with the date the snapshot was created.3,12,17All of this shifts the role of AI from a black-box oracle to a traceable assistant whose operations can be inspected and corrected by the human researcher.11,14,16Human-in-the-loop verification also extends to evaluating whether the set of references is complete and balanced.6,9AI systems, especially those trained on existing literature corpora, may inadvertently reinforce biases, under-representing certain regions, languages, or perspectives in their reference suggestions.2,9By exposing provenance and enabling inspection of the literature base used, AI tools allow researchers to notice and compensate for such biases, for instance by supplementing AI-suggested references with additional searches in domain databases or archives.11,12,16Institutions can support this by offering training that emphasises critical engagement with AI outputs, including checking citations, understanding the limits of AI tools, and recognising when to seek primary sources or expert input.6,8,9## 5. Durable Public Archives and the Mitigation of Reference Rot

5.1 The Problem of Reference Rot in Web-Based Scholarship

Reference rot, encompassing both link rot and content drift, poses a significant threat to the integrity and longevity of scholarly communication, particularly as web-based references become more common in academic papers.17,18,19Link rot occurs when a cited URL ceases to resolve, producing errors such as “404 – Page Not Found,” while content drift occurs when the content at a URL changes over time so that it no longer reflects the state or meaning that the citing author originally referenced.17,19Studies conducted under the Hiberlink project have analysed large corpora of full-text publications and found that a substantial proportion of web-at-large links in recent articles are rotten, with no archived version of the resource available within a short interval of the article’s publication date.17,19For science, technology, and medicine articles published between 2009 and 2012, one study estimated that roughly one in five contained at least one reference suffering from reference rot, meaning the cited web resource could not be reliably accessed as originally intended.17,19This degradation undermines the usability of scholarship by preventing readers, reviewers, and future researchers from examining cited evidence, replicating analyses, or understanding context, especially when the reference involves crucial materials such as datasets, software documentation, or policy documents hosted on the web.17The problem intensifies over time, as the probability of references rotting increases with the elapsed time since publication.17,19The phenomenon is particularly worrying in fields that rely heavily on rapidly changing web resources, including digital humanities, computational social science, and software engineering, where key artefacts may be blogs, code repositories, online APIs, or dynamically updated datasets.14,17Without systematic intervention, AI systems that automatically scrape, summarise, and cite web resources risk accelerating reference rot by generating large volumes of references that are not anchored in durable archives.3,12,17Effective mitigation requires acting at the moment when a web resource is first deemed significant by an author or system.17,19The Hiberlink project and related work have emphasised that the best time to prevent rot is when the reference is initially consulted and selected, by creating archival snapshots or “mementos” of the web page and recording their locations and creation dates.17,19This allows future readers to access the exact version viewed by the author, even if the live site later changes or disappears.17,19Such snapshots can be created by web archiving services that crawl and store content, but they can also be created on demand by authors, editors, or tools using services designed to preserve specific URLs.3,12,17Embedding this practice into AI-assisted workflows is therefore crucial if AI citation systems are to contribute positively to the durability of the scholarly record.3,11,12,17### 5.2 Perma.cc and Author-Initiated Link Preservation

Perma.cc is one of the most widely adopted tools for author-initiated link preservation, particularly in legal scholarship but increasingly across disciplines.3,17It provides a simple mechanism by which scholars, journals, courts, and other actors can create permanent records of web pages they cite, thereby combating link rot.3The workflow is straightforward: users identify the URL they wish to preserve, paste it into Perma.cc, and request the creation of a “Perma Link.”3Perma.cc then captures a snapshot of the page, stores it in its archival infrastructure, and returns a stable URL (the Perma Link) that points to an unalterable record of the page as it existed at the time of capture.3The original URL can still be cited, but the Perma Link provides a reliable backup that will not change or break.3Perma Links function as time capsules for web pages, providing readers with access to the content as the citing author saw it, regardless of subsequent changes to the live site.3,17Journals and courts increasingly encourage or require authors to use Perma Links in their citations, substituting or augmenting original URLs with the Perma.cc version to ensure long-term access.3,17This practice directly addresses both link rot and content drift by anchoring references in an archival snapshot rather than relying solely on the live web.3,17For AI systems that discover and cite web resources, integrating with Perma.cc’s API (or comparable services) would allow them to automatically generate archival links for every web URL cited, attaching the Perma Link and its creation date alongside the original URL in the provenance and citation records.3,17,19This integration would shift much of the burden of link preservation from individual authors to the AI tooling layer, making durable citation the default outcome of AI-assisted referencing.3,11,17### 5.3 Internet Archive Scholar and Domain-Scale Preservation

While Perma.cc focuses on author-initiated preservation of specific URLs, domain-scale web archiving initiatives such as Internet Archive Scholar aim to systematically capture and preserve large swaths of scholarly content on the public web.12,13Internet Archive Scholar provides full-text search over research publications archived in the Internet Archive’s collections, spanning natural sciences, humanities, biomedicine, industrial research, government reports, and more.12Its index includes content from multiple sources: public web content preserved in the Wayback Machine and Archive-It collections, digitised print materials, and general items in archive.org collections, all linked via a public bibliographic database known as fatcat.13Where possible, Internet Archive Scholar provides reader access to publications, including preprints or alternative versions if the final publisher version is not openly available.12,13Internet Archive Scholar’s mission aligns with the goal of mitigating reference rot for scholarly works themselves, as opposed to arbitrary web resources cited in papers.12,13,17By making automated attempts to capture and preserve all open-access research publications on the public web, it seeks to ensure that even if publisher sites go down, change hands, or alter URLs, archived versions of articles remain accessible.13,17Journals and repositories can also proactively register their content for inclusion in Internet Archive Scholar, though there may be delays between submission and appearance in the index.13For AI systems that rely on large corpora of scholarly articles, integrating with Internet Archive Scholar offers a way to bias retrieval toward archived copies that are likely to remain accessible, and to include archival URLs in citations or provenance records.11,12,13In addition, Internet Archive’s broader infrastructure, including the Wayback Machine, serves as a general-purpose web archive that may contain snapshots of many of the web resources cited in academic work, even if they were not explicitly captured through services like Perma.cc.3,12,17Some proposals for addressing reference rot suggest that publishers or platforms embed additional metadata in hyperlinks that record both the live URL and the archived URL with its creation date, enabling automated resolution to an archived version when the live site fails.17,19AI systems that generate or manage citations could adopt a similar approach by recording, for each web-based reference, both the original link and any available archival link from Internet Archive or Perma.cc, thereby maximising the likelihood that readers can retrieve the cited content in the future.3,12,17### 5.4 Combining Archives, RO-Crate, and Provenance for Durable AI Outputs

The infrastructures and practices discussed above—Perma.cc, Internet Archive Scholar, RO-Crate, PROV-O, and the PID layer—can be combined to create robust, durable records of AI-assisted research outputs.1,3,11,14,16,17,20A scholarly AI system could, for each project, construct an RO-Crate that contains the main outputs (draft manuscripts, analysis scripts, datasets), the provenance metadata (modeled in PROV-O), and the full set of references, including PIDs, live URLs, and archival links.14,16,20For web-based resources, the system could automatically generate Perma Links at the time they are first referenced and record both the original URL and the Perma.cc URL with timestamp in the crate’s metadata.3,17For articles, it could include DOIs and, where available, Internet Archive Scholar URLs or other archival links.1,12,13,17Once assembled, such an RO-Crate could be deposited in an institutional repository or subject archive, where it becomes part of the institution’s long-term digital preservation strategy.14,16The Root Data Entity would carry metadata such as title, description, creators (with ORCID), institutional affiliations (with ROR), project RAiD, license, and links to related outputs (including DOIs for published versions of the work).1,5,16The provenance graph would document how AI tools were used in the research process, satisfying institutional disclosure requirements and offering future readers insight into the role of AI in the study.6,9,20By storing both the crate and its embedded archival links in durable repositories and web archives, institutions can guard against loss of key resources and ensure that AI-assisted scholarship remains interpretable over the long term.12,14,16,17For AI systems operating in this mode, durability is no longer an afterthought but a design constraint: the system must be able to surface, export, and archive not just text but the full context of sources and transformations that led to that text.11,14,16,20This encourages a shift away from purely ephemeral chat interfaces toward workflows that treat AI interactions as components in a broader ecosystem of version-controlled manuscripts, curated datasets, archival URLs, and reproducible workflows.11,14,16Higher-education institutions, in turn, can support and reinforce these practices by selecting and endorsing AI tools that integrate with archival and provenance infrastructures, and by educating students and researchers about their use.6,8,9,11## 6. End-to-End Workflow Patterns for Higher-Education Research

6.1 Repository-Centred AI Workflows

In research-intensive universities, institutional repositories and current research information systems (CRIS) already form the backbone of scholarly information management, aggregating publications, datasets, and sometimes software with links to ORCID, ROR, RAiD, and DOIs.1,5,16A repository-centred AI workflow would position AI systems as interfaces and assistants that operate in close coordination with these repositories, rather than as standalone tools detached from institutional infrastructure.11,16Researchers would log into AI tools using institutional identities linked to ORCID, enabling the system to fetch and update the researcher’s publication and project records from the repository and CRIS.1,5,11When AI tools assist with literature reviews or drafting, they would preferentially draw on open-access content indexed in institutional repositories or Internet Archive Scholar, ensuring that cited sources are accessible to the institution’s community.11,12,16As the research project progresses, the AI system would accumulate provenance logs and intermediate artefacts—notes, code snippets, data transformations—packaging them periodically into RO-Crates.14,16,20These crates could be stored in the institutional repository as “working objects” during the project and then curated into more formal datasets or software packages upon publication.14,16Once a manuscript reaches a stable form, the AI tool could assist with finalising the reference list, ensuring that all cited articles, datasets, and software have complete citations with DOIs and other PIDs where available, and that web-based references have corresponding Perma Links.1,3,4,7,11It could also generate a provenance summary and AI-use disclosure paragraph, drawing from the PROV-O logs.6,9,20Depositing the final RO-Crate—containing the manuscript, data, code, and provenance—into the repository would then become a standard part of project completion.14,16From the institution’s perspective, such workflows enhance the quality and transparency of repository holdings.14,16Each deposited RO-Crate would include not only the final outputs but also machine-readable metadata about their relationships, creators, institutions, and projects, structured using RO-Crate, PROV-O, and PIDs.1,14,16,20This supports not only discovery and reuse but also reporting and assessment, as CRIS systems can query the metadata to understand how AI tools are being used across the institution’s research portfolio.1,5,16It also creates a foundation for future analyses of AI’s impact on scholarly communication, as the provenance records provide empirical data on AI-assisted practices.11,16,20### 6.2 Teaching, Theses, and Student Research

Higher-education workflows are not limited to professional research; they also encompass undergraduate and graduate student projects, theses, and coursework, where AI tools are rapidly becoming ubiquitous.6,8,9Institutions have begun to issue policies specifying how students may and may not use AI in assignments and dissertations, typically requiring disclosure of AI use and cautioning against uncritical reliance on AI-generated content.6,8,9In this context, scholarly AI systems that support provenance capture, proper citation, and durable archiving can serve as pedagogical tools, teaching students good scholarly habits from the outset.6,8,9For example, a thesis-writing assistant integrated with the institutional repository could guide students in constructing reference lists that include data and software, using standard citation templates.4,7It could also prompt students to archive web sources via Perma.cc and to include details of AI use in their methods sections.3,6,9For student research projects that yield reusable datasets or code, AI tools could help package outputs into RO-Crates suitable for deposit in the institutional repository or teaching collections.14,16The RO-Crate metadata could include the student’s ORCID, supervisory relationships, and course or programme identifiers, as well as PROV-O-based provenance records of how AI tools were used in the project.1,16,20This not only facilitates reuse of student-generated materials in future teaching and research but also models best practices around FAIR data and transparent AI use.14,16Librarians and research-support staff could then use these crates as exemplars in training sessions on data management, reproducibility, and responsible AI, illustrating how provenance, PIDs, and archiving come together in concrete student work.6,8,9,16### 6.3 Governance, Policies, and Training

Implementing such integrated workflows requires coordinated governance, clear policies, and ongoing training at the institutional level.6,8,9Universities must articulate policies that specify acceptable uses of AI in research and teaching, outline expectations for disclosure and provenance documentation, and clarify how AI-related metadata will be stored and used.6,8,9These policies should align with external guidance from style manuals and disciplinary bodies, which generally agree that AI cannot be an author, that AI use must be fully disclosed, and that AI must not be used to fabricate or falsify data.2,6,9,10Institutions also need to decide how AI tools themselves are to be cited or acknowledged in theses, publications, and reports, ensuring consistency across departments.6,9,10Training programmes are essential to equip researchers and students with the skills to use AI tools responsibly and to understand the provenance and archiving infrastructures with which these tools interact.6,8,9Library-led workshops can introduce participants to PIDs (ORCID, ROR, RAiD, DOIs), data and software citation principles, RO-Crate packaging, and services like Perma.cc and Internet Archive Scholar.1,3,4,7,12,14,16They can also demonstrate AI tools integrated with this infrastructure, showing how provenance logs are generated, how reference lists are constructed and verified, and how final outputs are deposited into repositories.6,8,9,11Emphasising that authors remain responsible for verifying AI-suggested sources and that they must not treat AI as a shortcut around proper literature reading and evaluation is critical.2,6,9Governance also involves technology selection and procurement.6,8,9Institutions should favour AI tools that support provenance capture, PID integration, RO-Crate export, and archival link generation, and they should push vendors to adopt open standards like PROV-O and RO-Crate.14,16,20Open-source platforms like Manubot illustrate how transparent, version-controlled manuscript authoring can be combined with automated citation handling and continuous integration, offering a model for AI-augmented writing environments.11By choosing tools that align with these principles, universities can steer AI adoption in a direction that strengthens rather than weakens the scholarly record.6,8,9,11,16## 7. Conclusion: Designing AI Systems as Stewards of the Scholarly Record

The integration of generative AI into higher-education research workflows is not a temporary fad but a structural shift in how scholarship is conducted, documented, and communicated.6,8,9Whether AI systems ultimately enhance or erode the integrity of the scholarly record depends on whether they are designed and governed as stewards of provenance, citation, and archival durability, or as opaque text generators indifferent to these concerns.6,9,11This report has argued that the building blocks for responsible AI integration already exist in the form of persistent identifiers for people, organisations, projects, and outputs; provenance ontologies like PROV-O; research object packaging formats such as RO-Crate; and data and software citation principles.1,4,5,7,14,16,20By leveraging these, scholarly AI systems can capture rich provenance of prompts, sources, and transformations, generate complete and standards-compliant reference lists that encompass data and software, and anchor web-based references in durable public archives like Perma.cc and Internet Archive Scholar.1,3,4,7,11,12,14,16,17,20At the provenance level, AI systems should model each generative interaction as a PROV-O activity that uses inputs (prompts, retrieved sources) and generates outputs (texts, code), with clear attribution to both human and AI agents.6,9,20This provenance can be exported in RO-Crate packages alongside the research artefacts themselves, creating self-describing bundles that can be archived, reused, and analysed.14,16At the citation level, AI systems must move beyond hallucinating references toward integration with bibliographic and PID infrastructures, ensuring that every suggested citation resolves to real, verifiable works and that data and software are cited in line with community principles.1,4,7,11They should assist authors in acknowledging AI use within methods or acknowledgements sections and, where appropriate, generating reference entries for AI tools as software or personal communication, consistent with style-guideline recommendations.2,4,6,9,10On the archival front, AI systems should automate the preservation of web-based references by invoking services like Perma.cc at the time of citation and by incorporating archival URLs and creation dates into provenance and reference metadata.3,17,19They should preferentially retrieve and cite articles from durable archives such as Internet Archive Scholar, ensuring that the literature base they rely on remains accessible.11,12,13When combined with institutional repositories and CRIS systems, these practices support end-to-end workflows in which AI-assisted scholarship is securely anchored in the institution’s long-term preservation infrastructure, complete with provenance, PIDs, and archival links.1,5,11,14,16,20Ultimately, the success of these approaches depends on institutional governance and human practice.6,8,9Universities must craft policies that mandate disclosure of AI use, reinforce human responsibility for verification, and encourage the adoption of tools that support provenance and archival standards.6,8,9Training programmes must equip researchers and students with the knowledge to critically engage with AI outputs, understand the importance of PIDs and RO-Crate, and use archiving services effectively.4,6,7,8,9,14,16If these socio-technical elements are aligned, scholarly AI systems can become powerful allies in preserving source provenance, maintaining complete and accurate reference lists, and building durable public archives, thereby strengthening the scholarly record in the era of artificial intelligence rather than allowing it to fray.3,6,9,11,14,16,17,20
