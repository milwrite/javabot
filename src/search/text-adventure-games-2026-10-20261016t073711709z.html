AI and Text Adventure Games in the Age of Generative Storytelling

The convergence of artificial intelligence and text adventure games marks a pivotal moment in the evolution of interactive fiction, transforming parser-based puzzles and branching narratives into open-ended, dynamically generated worlds that respond fluidly to player input.2,3,6,9Classic titles such as Zork defined the grammar and logic of text-based exploration using hand-authored parsers, discrete game states, and carefully crafted puzzles, while contemporary systems like AI Dungeon, Perchance, Infinite Worlds, and Wit's End employ large language models (LLMs) to improvise narrative content in real time across any genre.2,3,6,9,12,14,18This shift introduces new creative possibilities—such as limitless branching, voice-driven play, and AI-controlled game masters—but also raises technical and design challenges related to coherence, commonsense reasoning, state tracking, and player agency, all of which are now active topics of research in interactive storytelling and digital narrative design.10,11,15,19,20By tracing the history of text adventures, examining current AI-powered platforms, and exploring emerging research on LLM-driven story generation and game mastering, we can understand both the opportunities and constraints that define AI’s role in the next generation of text-based games.6,9,10,11,15,19,20## From Interactive Fiction to AI-Driven Worlds

Text adventures are part of the broader genre of interactive fiction, in which a game world and its unfolding story are described primarily in text and the player influences events through typed or selected commands.2,4,16The term Interactive Fiction itself was coined in 1979 by programmer Robert Lafore to describe computer games whose narrative and environment are presented as text while the player actively shapes the course of events.16In classic text adventures, the interactivity typically takes the form of puzzle-solving and exploration: players read a description of a location, decide what to do, and enter a command that the game parses and executes, advancing the story or revealing new parts of the world.2,4,7,8,16Early systems like Adventure (also known as Colossal Cave Adventure) and Zork relied on custom parsers and handcrafted databases of rooms, items, and puzzles, granting the illusion of infinite possibilities while carefully constraining the set of recognized commands and valid world states.2,4,5,7,8Modern AI text adventure platforms retain the textual interface but shift from fixed content to generative storytelling, using machine-learned models to create narrative continuations and adapt to player input on the fly.6,9,12,14,18AI Dungeon, for example, allows players to type any action or line of dialogue and then uses an underlying language model to produce the next segment of the story, with no predetermined win condition or scripted plot; the result is an effectively unbounded narrative space that can span fantasy, science fiction, noir, or any other genre.6,9,12,18Perchance’s AI text adventure system, Infinite Worlds, and Wit's End similarly leverage generative AI to build dynamic adventures, though they differ in interface, modality, and the degree of structural guidance they impose on the narrative.1,9,12,13,14In parallel, research on AI-driven interactive narrative explores how LLMs can be embedded into game environments such as Minecraft as conversational non-player characters (NPCs) or game masters, enabling open-ended spoken dialogue and emergent mystery-solving while still respecting safety, coherence, and character consistency.10,19The move from parser-centric interactive fiction to LLM-based AI adventures can be understood as a transition from grammar-bound simulation to probabilistic narrative improvisation, reframing the design space from “what commands does the game recognize?” to “what stories can the model plausibly invent?”.2,6,15,19Yet the fundamental design problem remains remarkably similar: how to provide a world that feels manipulable and responsive, create a story that remains coherent over time, and give players meaningful agency without overwhelming them or breaking the fiction.2,7,8,16The difference lies in the toolset: instead of MDL or Z-machine bytecode, designers now work with prompt engineering, context windows, and multi-agent orchestration around LLMs, while still inheriting lessons from decades of interactive fiction about pacing, puzzle integration, and the psychology of choice.3,4,15,17,19,20## Historical Foundations of Text Adventure Games

From Colossal Cave to Zork and Infocom

The origins of text adventure games are usually traced to Will Crowther’s Colossal Cave Adventure (c. 1975), a Fortran program that combined a simulation of cave exploration with simple textual descriptions and two-word commands.4,7,8Don Woods expanded and modified Crowther’s program in 1977, disseminating the game widely on mainframe systems and establishing Adventure as the prototype for text-based computer games organized as interactive stories.4,7,8Inspired by Adventure but dissatisfied with its limited command language, a group of students and staff in MIT’s Dynamic Modelling Group—Tim Anderson, Marc Blank, Bruce Daniels, and Dave Lebling—began developing a more sophisticated interactive fiction game in 1977 using the MDL programming language, a descendant of Lisp associated with artificial intelligence research.2,4,5MDL’s expressiveness allowed the Zork team to build a rich database of objects and rooms—roughly 400 items and hundreds of locations in the final mainframe version—while implementing a more capable parser that could understand a wider variety of sentences than Adventure’s simple verb–noun syntax.2,4,5,7The game they produced, initially known colloquially as Zork (MIT hacker jargon for an unfinished program), quickly achieved cult status across American universities via ARPANET, as computer science students logged into the MIT PDP-10 to play through the sprawling Great Underground Empire.2,4,5The developers later attempted to rename the completed game to Dungeon, but by then the nonsense name Zork had stuck among players and was retained in the commercial release.5,7In 1979, several members of the Zork team founded Infocom as a software company to commercialize text adventure games on emerging personal computers, recognizing that the growing microcomputer market offered a far larger audience than time-shared mainframes.2,3,4,5,7Memory limitations on home machines, however, required splitting the original Zork into three parts: Zork: The Great Underground Empire – Part I (later Zork I) in 1980, Zork II: The Wizard of Frobozz in 1981, and Zork III: The Dungeon Master in 1982.2,3,5,7,8To achieve cross-platform compatibility, Infocom developed the Zork Implementation Language (ZIL), a derivative of MDL that compiled into bytecode for a standardized virtual machine known as the Z-machine; by porting only the interpreter to each architecture, they could release their games simultaneously on Apple II, Atari 8-bit systems, IBM PC compatibles, Commodore 64, and many other platforms.3,5Zork I was first published by Personal Software for the TRS-80 in December 1980, after which Infocom bought back the rights and self-published the trilogy beginning in late 1981.2,3,5The game became a major commercial success: the first episode sold more than 38,000 copies in 1982 and about 150,000 in 1984, while the trilogy collectively sold over 680,000 copies through 1986, accounting for roughly one-third of Infocom’s two million game sales.2,3,4,5Historians and critics have since regarded Zork as one of the greatest games of all time, noting its foundational role in the adventure game genre and its influence on multi-user dungeons (MUDs) and massively multiplayer online role-playing games.3,4,5,8Its cultural presence persists today, appearing as a reference or playable Easter egg in contemporary media such as Ready Player One, The Martian, and even the Call of Duty: Black Ops series.5,8### Infocom’s Broader Interactive Fiction Legacy

Infocom leveraged the technical infrastructure built for Zork to create a large catalog of interactive fiction titles, each using variants of the same Z-machine interpreter and sophisticated text parser.3,7,8Their games shared a core pattern: the user enters short strings of words to direct actions, the program responds with a description of the world or the outcome, and the player iteratively reads, decides, and types again, forming a loop of narration and agency.3,7,16In addition to the Zork trilogy, Infocom published critically acclaimed adventures such as Deadline (1982), Starcross (1982), and The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy (1984), the latter developed in collaboration with author Douglas Adams.3,7,8Infocom’s writing style was often praised as spare yet wry, balancing minimalist descriptions with humor and mind-challenging puzzles that required careful attention and lateral thinking.7,8The company also became known for elaborate physical packaging, including “feelies”—maps, artifacts, and documents related to the game world—that reinforced immersion and provided clues, exemplified by products like A Mind Forever Voyaging, a 1986 title that used a simulated city and an artificial intelligence protagonist (PRISM) to explore socio-political themes in a virtual world.7,8While these games were entirely scripted rather than AI-generated, they demonstrated that text alone could support rich, emotionally resonant worlds with complex systems, a lesson that underlies many contemporary ambitions for AI-driven storytelling.7,8,20By the late 1980s, however, graphical role-playing games such as Ultima and Might and Magic eclipsed text adventures in popularity, contributing to Infocom’s financial difficulties and eventual acquisition by Activision in 1986, followed by the shut-down of the Infocom division in 1989.3,5,7,8Despite the decline, interactive fiction experienced a resurgence in the 1990s and beyond, driven by hobbyist authors and tools like Inform, a natural-language-based programming language designed for writing interactive fiction.17,16This independent movement preserved the parser-based tradition and continued to experiment with narrative structure, establishing a design vocabulary that now intersects with AI-generated text adventures in important ways.16,17## Design Principles of Classic Parser-Based Adventures

Parsers, Commands, and the Illusion of Infinite Choice

Classic text adventures are built on a tension between the illusion of unlimited choice and the underlying reality of finite, parser-recognized commands.2,7,8,16In games like Adventure and Zork, players type instructions such as “go north,” “take lamp,” or “open door,” relying on a parser that interprets these strings, maps them to internal actions, and modifies the game state accordingly.2,4,7Early parsers typically understood only simple verb–noun patterns, but Infocom’s systems evolved to handle more complex constructions, allowing multiple objects, prepositions, and nuanced phrasing, thereby increasing the sense that the game could “understand” natural language.3,4,5In practice, designers constrained the action space by defining a limited set of verbs and specific uses of objects, yet the sparse textual descriptions created a wide imaginative canvas that made the world feel larger and more flexible than its mechanical implementation.7,8As The Strong Museum notes in its reflection on Dungeon (a renamed version of the mainframe Zork), this interface could, “in the hands of a good programmer and writer, provide the illusion of a world of infinite possibilities” despite actually limiting players to manageable sets of recognized commands.7The challenge was to balance guidance and discovery: too few recognized actions or overly obscure puzzles could frustrate players, while too much constraint could shatter the illusion of freedom.7,8,16### World Modeling, Puzzles, and Narrative Structure

Underneath the textual surface, parser-based adventures rely on explicit world models consisting of rooms, items, characters, and puzzle states, often implemented as databases or object graphs.2,4,5,7In Zork, MDL facilitated the construction of roughly 400 distinct objects organized into a coherent spatial structure—the Great Underground Empire—comprising mazes, temples, dams, and varied locales connected by directional links and scripted transitions.2,4,5,8Each object could have properties such as location, visibility, open or closed state, and special behaviors; puzzles emerged from the interactions of these properties, requiring players to reason about both physical configuration and narrative clues.2,4,8The narrative in classic adventures is generally semi-linear, with a core progression gated by puzzles and key items, accompanied by optional side areas and treasures that reward exploration.2,4,8For instance, in the Zork trilogy, players pursue treasures and seek to become the Dungeon Master while traversing intricate mazes and fantastical regions such as the Land of the Dead and the Land of Shadows, encountering wizards, thieves, and hazards like the infamous grue.2,4,8Puzzles often hinge on lateral thinking, wordplay, and environmental observation, making the act of reading itself central to play; clues are embedded in room descriptions, object names, and occasional narrative hints.7,8These design principles—parser interpretation, explicit state tracking, gated progression, and puzzle-based agency—provide a framework that contrasts sharply with many AI-generated text adventures, which rely on probabilistic language models rather than deterministic world state graphs, and on narrative continuation rather than scripted puzzles.6,9,15,19Understanding the strengths and limitations of classic designs helps clarify where AI advances can add flexibility and richness, and where they risk undermining coherence or player agency if not carefully constrained.15,19,20## Emergence of AI-Powered Text Adventure Platforms

AI Dungeon and Open-Ended Generative Storytelling

AI Dungeon, developed by Latitude and first released in 2019, is one of the most prominent AI text adventure platforms and a key example of how generative models have reshaped the genre.6,9,18Initially created by Nick Walton during a hackathon at Brigham Young University’s Perception, Control, and Cognition deep learning lab, the first version used an early variant of OpenAI’s GPT family to generate textual content based on player input.6A full version built on the “complete” GPT-2 model was released on Google Colaboratory in December 2019, then ported as a cross-platform web application later that month, with subsequent reformulations of the underlying AI model in July 2020.6AI Dungeon’s core design diverges sharply from classical parser adventures: rather than a fixed database of rooms and puzzles, it offers a blank text box into which players can type any action, line of dialogue, or narrative beat, and then uses an AI model to write the next chunk of the story.6,9,12,18There is no predefined script or win condition; instead, the narrative is “go-anywhere,” adapting flexibly to choices such as shifting genres mid-story—from a cyberpunk heist to a detective thriller when a player betrays their crew—and generating new characters and plotlines around those decisions.12,14,18AI Dungeon supports custom scenarios, community-made worlds, and preset adventures, along with tools such as Story Cards and Plot Essentials that allow players to feed structured information about characters and settings to keep the AI more consistent.12,18However, the quality of AI Dungeon’s storytelling is highly dependent on which AI model is available under a given subscription tier.18Reviews note that on the free tier, which uses smaller or less capable models, stories can drift off-topic, contradict their own continuity, or repeat passages in longer sessions, reflecting known challenges of long-form generation.15,18Higher-tier plans unlock stronger models and larger memory windows, resulting in narratives that hold together significantly better and can feel like collaboration with a human writer.18Even so, players report issues such as the AI taking control of character emotions instead of leaving them to the player, or varying levels of content moderation, leading some writers to prefer alternative platforms like NovelAI for more controlled storytelling.12,18### Perchance, Infinite Worlds, and No-Signup AI Adventures

Beyond AI Dungeon, a growing ecosystem of AI text adventure platforms offers different trade-offs between accessibility, structure, and narrative freedom.1,9,13,14Perchance AI’s text adventure mode, accessible via a simple web URL, runs a free, no-signup AI text adventure directly in the browser, with no limits on playtime or story length, making it attractive to users who want to experiment without commitment.1,9,13As described in community discussions, Perchance AI’s RPG-like environments can resemble open worlds where the AI responds to almost any input, although these experiences often lack a clear endgame and instead emphasize perpetual exploration and improvisation.1Infinite Worlds, another web-based platform, is positioned as a tool for “world-builders,” allowing players to start for free and then purchase extra credits to extend their stories.9It emphasizes creating expansive settings and persistent characters, appealing to users who enjoy the creative process of designing universes rather than following pre-authored plots.9Both Perchance and Infinite Worlds illustrate how generative AI can lower the barrier to entry for text adventures by reducing setup friction and enabling continuous, on-demand play, while still inheriting the same coherence and state-tracking challenges that affect AI Dungeon.9,13,15### Wit's End and Voice-Driven AI Game Mastering

Wit’s End, delivered through the Weekend app, represents a hybrid approach that combines AI-driven narrative generation with a live human game master, focusing on group play via smart TVs.9,12,14In Wit’s End, players describe their characters out loud, and the game master constructs attributes and backstories on the spot, while an AI system generates fresh locations, characters, and plot twists for each session.12,14The experience is voice-driven: players speak their actions into a microphone, and the game master narrates immediate consequences, creating a fast-moving, living narrative where there is no preset story tree and choices genuinely shape the unfolding events.12,14By design, Wit’s End emphasizes narrative momentum and social engagement over puzzle density or long-term continuity, leveraging AI to ensure that sessions never feel like the same quest twice while relying on human oversight to maintain pacing and resolve ambiguities.12,14Comparisons between Wit’s End and AI Dungeon note that while AI Dungeon wins on sheer genre range and solo flexibility, Wit’s End often provides stronger narrative momentum and a more structured sense of stakes, making it a better fit for groups seeking a guided adventure rather than sandbox improvisation.12This human-in-the-loop approach aligns with research recommendations that AI game master systems be explainable, controllable, and grounded, with human oversight to mitigate issues of coherence and bias.19,20## Technical Foundations: Large Language Models and Long-Form Story Generation

Large Language Models and Narrative Generation

Modern AI text adventures rely heavily on large language models (LLMs)—deep neural networks trained on massive corpora of text to predict the next token in a sequence—leveraging their ability to generate coherent sentences and paragraphs conditioned on prompts.6,9,10,15When a player enters an action or line of dialogue, the system typically constructs a composite prompt that includes recent story context, character information, and sometimes structured metadata (such as Story Cards or knowledge bases), then feeds this prompt to an LLM to produce the next segment of the narrative.6,9,12,18The same mechanism underlies experimental systems like the Minecraft murder mystery described by Ma et al., which combines GPT-4o with automatic speech recognition (ASR) and text-to-speech (TTS) so players can converse naturally with LLM-driven NPCs.10LLMs excel at short- to medium-length narrative generation, especially when given well-structured prompts that constrain genre, character tone, and desired outcomes.10,15,20However, long story generation—stories exceeding 1,000 words or extended multi-session adventures—remains challenging due to issues of discourse coherence, plot consistency, and attention degradation across long context windows.15As Liu and colleagues and related work have noted, as the length of generated text increases, LLM outputs are prone to repetition, hallucination, and topic drift, especially when they must recall long-distance key information such as events, characters, and relationships.15### Multi-Agent Frameworks and Storywriter

To address these challenges, recent research proposes multi-agent frameworks that distribute narrative responsibilities across specialized modules rather than relying on a single monolithic model.15The STORYWRITER framework, for instance, uses multiple agents to plan overarching plot arcs, track character states, and generate individual scenes, with coordination mechanisms that aim to maintain coherence and completeness over long-form narratives.15Evaluations indicate that STORYWRITER can significantly outperform baselines in both story quality and length, generating longer stories while still scoring higher on relevance and coherence metrics than systems such as DOC or GPT-4o-mini alone.15Nevertheless, even STORYWRITER experiences inevitable coherence decline as narratives grow longer, reflecting the general pattern that longer texts increase the probability of inconsistency and drift.15Context fragmentation and attention degradation become particularly severe once input sequences exceed approximately 10,000 characters, at which point models struggle to maintain narrative focus and recall earlier plot developments, often producing off-topic or incoherent outputs.15These empirical observations have direct implications for AI text adventures, where players may engage in multi-hour sessions and expect the game to remember past events, relationships, and unresolved plots.6,9,18### Commonsense Reasoning, State Tracking, and Evaluation

Beyond coherence, AI text adventures must contend with commonsense reasoning and explicit state tracking, both of which are central to maintaining believable worlds.15,19Zamora-Martínez and colleagues, in their study of automated game mastering, identify several core challenges: extracting player actions from conversational input, validating whether those actions are feasible in the fictional context, and thoroughly tracking items and characters to preserve user agency.19While LLMs perform reasonably well on tasks such as item tracking and map design—maintaining where objects are and describing coherent spatial layouts—they often struggle with commonsense reasoning, especially in scenarios that require distinguishing between actions possible in the real world and those compatible with the fictional world’s rules.19Their evaluation framework introduces categories such as GM-P (game master planning) and GM-P-GM (planning plus game master validation), showing that LLMs like ChatGPT and Bard can provide satisfying gaming experiences when players choose from suggested actions, but perform poorly when required to improvise new scenes and enforce consistency.19Performance on commonsense validation remains particularly low, aligning with broader findings that such reasoning is one of the notable weaknesses of current LLMs.19,15Moreover, these models tend to irregularly move the story forward, sometimes narrating single events and at other times long scenes, and often lack diversity in plot themes, with repetitive events and locations emerging after a few hours of play.19These technical limitations underscore why AI text adventure platforms need careful design strategies, such as bounded session length, structured prompts, external memory modules, and human oversight, to mitigate the risk of incoherent or unsatisfying narratives, especially in open-ended, sandbox-style experiences.15,18,19,20## AI as Game Master: LLM-Driven Narration and NPCs

Modeling the Game Master’s Role

In tabletop role-playing games (TTRPGs), the game master (GM) is responsible for designing the world, narrating events, managing NPCs, and adjudicating player actions, a multifaceted role that encompasses storytelling, simulation, and social facilitation.19Zamora-Martínez et al. argue that automating this role is a major challenge for natural language processing and AI, given its complexity in dialogue management, creativity, and commonsense reasoning.19They outline a long-term goal of modeling the diverse set of skills needed to play as a GM, including world and story design, understanding player input, reasoning about action feasibility, and tracking game state for items and characters.19From an interactive storytelling perspective, these responsibilities intersect directly with the design of AI text adventures and AI-driven narrative games such as Wit’s End and LLM-based mystery experiences.10,12,19A GM-like system must not only generate vivid descriptions and dialogue but also enforce the fiction’s internal logic, decide when to reveal information, and manage pacing, all while supporting user agency.19,20If an AI GM simply adjusts the narrative to accommodate any player input, without asserting constraints or maintaining mystery, players may feel that their actions lack meaningful consequences, or that the story lacks tension and structure.19### LLM-Driven NPCs in Minecraft Murder Mystery

Ma et al.’s Minecraft murder mystery project illustrates one approach to integrating LLMs into game environments as conversational characters rather than full GMs.10In this system, GPT-4o is combined with ASR and TTS to create speech-enabled NPCs that inhabit a Minecraft world where players attempt to solve a mystery through natural dialogue.10Prompt engineering techniques are used to enforce character consistency, safety filters, and narrative depth, ensuring that NPC responses align with predefined roles, backstories, and motivations.10Evaluations of the system show improved performance in character correctness, dialogue coherence, and safety, with statistically significant gains in safety and character consistency (p = 0.00195), demonstrating that careful prompt design and role conditioning can mitigate some of the uncontrolled generative behavior of LLMs.10However, the NPCs operate within a relatively bounded scenario—a specific murder mystery—where designers can define constraints and reference knowledge, making the problem more tractable than open-ended GMing across limitless worlds.10,19Still, this work offers a template for AI text adventures that incorporate voice-driven NPCs and quest-givers, potentially augmenting classic text interfaces with conversational agents and multimodal interactions.10,20### Hybrid Human–AI Game Mastering

Platforms like Wit’s End show that combining AI narrative generation with a human game master can yield experiences that benefit from both flexibility and structure.12,14In Wit’s End, the live GM interprets spoken player actions, manages pacing, and ensures that the AI-generated content remains engaging and coherent, while the AI provides fresh storylines, characters, and outcomes for each session.12,14This division of labor addresses some of the difficulties identified in GM evaluation studies—such as maintaining mystery, enforcing constraints, and avoiding repetitive plots—by relying on human judgment for high-level narrative decisions and using AI for improvisational detail and variation.19,20Research on game mastering suggests that human-in-the-loop models are likely to remain important, especially in contexts where explainability, grounding, and controllability are critical to player trust and enjoyment.19,20AI systems can assist by suggesting events, generating dialogue, or tracking objects, but final adjudication of ambiguous actions and narrative significance may need a human GM, particularly in social tabletop settings.19,20This perspective informs design choices in AI text adventures, where different degrees of autonomy and oversight can be configured depending on whether the experience is solo, group-based, or educational.11,12,20## Player Experience, Agency, and Coherence in AI Text Adventures

Agency and Choice in Text-Based Narratives

Player agency—the feeling that one’s choices matter and shape the story—is central to both classic and AI-driven text adventures.7,8,12,14,16In traditional parser-based games, agency is grounded in a well-defined world model and puzzle structure: players explore, experiment, and discover solutions, with clear feedback when actions succeed or fail.2,4,7,8Although commands are limited, the illusion of a vast interactive world emerges from the richness of descriptions and the non-linear layout of rooms and items.7,8AI text adventures introduce a different form of agency by allowing nearly any textual input as a possible action, shifting the boundary from “what the parser understands” to “what the model can plausibly narrate.”6,9,12,18This nearly unconstrained input space can be freeing, particularly for creative players who enjoy improvisation and genre-bending, as AI Dungeon illustrates with its capacity to adapt to radical mid-story changes.12,14,18However, it also raises questions about meaningful choice: if the AI will accept and adjust to any action, without fixed stakes or failure states, players may struggle to discern the significance of their decisions.18,19Reviews of AI Dungeon note that the AI sometimes decides what a character feels instead of letting the player define emotions, subtly reducing agency over interiority, while the lack of structured goals can be both liberating and frustrating.12,18The variability of plot direction and pacing can further complicate agency, as LLMs may irregularly move the story forward or retroactively reinterpret events.15,19Designers therefore experiment with scaffolding—such as Story Cards and quest frameworks—to give players more control over the narrative arc while still leveraging AI flexibility.12,18,20### Coherence, Repetition, and Content Quality

Coherence and consistency are recurring concerns in AI text adventures, especially in long sessions.15,18,19As empirical studies show, LLMs experience context fragmentation when input sequences grow very long, leading to off-topic outputs or failure to recall earlier plot developments.15This manifests in practice in AI Dungeon as story drift, contradictions, and repetition, particularly on smaller models and free tiers.18Players report that in extended play, the AI can loop descriptions, resurrect defeated characters, or forget important decisions, undermining the sense of a stable narrative world.12,18Zamora-Martínez et al. similarly observe that LLM-based GM agents exhibit limited diversity of plots and locations over multi-hour tests, with events and places repeating and an absence of mystery due to the AI’s tendency to simply accommodate whatever the player says.19These issues diminish the perceived quality of the story and highlight the need for explicit state tracking and higher-level narrative control mechanisms, such as external memory, symbolic world models, or multi-agent planners.15,19Some platforms address this by limiting session length, segmenting adventures into episodes, or using metadata to track key facts and relationships across turns.11,15,18### Engagement and Comprehensibility in Educational Contexts

The educational potential of AI-generated interactive fiction adds another dimension to player experience, as clarity and coherence are prerequisites for learning.11,20Rogosch and Schrader’s pilot study on AI-generated interactive fiction for higher education uses a domain-agnostic pipeline to generate STEM-focused scenarios and then asks participants to rate them on narrative clarity, story-content coherence, engagement, and length acceptance.11The results show relatively positive ratings for narrative clarity and acceptable length, with engagement hovering near the neutral midpoint; however, story-content coherence is notably weaker than other dimensions.11Qualitative feedback indicates that integration of quiz elements within the narrative is a bottleneck: abrupt setting changes, artificial in-story motivation for quiz prompts, and missing story-level consequences for wrong answers reduce coherence and immersion.11These observations parallel issues in entertainment-focused AI text adventures, where sudden tone shifts or unexplained events can break immersion, and where actions lacking meaningful consequences can feel hollow.18,19For educational applications, designers must therefore pay particular attention to aligning assessment tasks with the story’s internal logic, embedding quizzes as natural narrative events rather than external interruptions.11,20The study’s design implications suggest that AI interactive fiction can be useful in education if narrative clarity is maintained and coherence improved, particularly through better integration of learning objectives and world-building.11This reinforces the broader lesson that AI-generated text adventures require not just technical generation capabilities but careful narrative design, especially when used for purposes beyond entertainment.11,20## Educational and Creative Applications of AI-Generated Interactive Fiction

Interactive Fiction as a Learning Medium

Interactive fiction has long been considered a promising medium for learning, thanks to its capacity to embed complex scenarios, ethical dilemmas, and problem-solving tasks within engaging narratives.11,16,20With generative AI, educators can theoretically produce tailored scenarios at scale, customizing stories to specific domains, student levels, or learning objectives.11,20Rogosch and Schrader’s study demonstrates a pipeline for generating such scenarios and evaluating them for comprehensibility and engagement, offering initial evidence that AI-created interactive fiction can be understood and accepted by STEM students, albeit with room for improvement in coherence.11The key design challenge lies in aligning interactivity with learning: choices must matter both narratively and pedagogically, with consequences that reinforce concepts rather than distract from them.11,20For example, an AI-generated lab simulation might ask students to choose experimental steps, with the narrative reflecting the outcomes and embedding explanations when mistakes occur, thereby blending assessment with feedback.11,20Integrating these elements into a coherent story requires careful prompt engineering and structural planning, echoing the requirements of educational games and serious games more broadly.11,20### Digital Storytelling and the Creative Promise of AI

Carolyn Handler Miller’s Digital Storytelling (fifth edition) situates generative AI within a broader landscape of interactive entertainment, emphasizing its “creative promise” for new forms of storytelling and experiential media.20The book highlights projects that utilize generative AI to craft dynamic narratives, including VR experiences and interactive worlds that respond to user behavior in complex ways.20Industry practitioners such as Roger Holzberg and Kate Pullinger note that such work recognizes VR and digital media as storytelling mediums with profound human impact, where AI tools can assist creators in designing rich, responsive experiences.20Within this context, AI text adventures can be seen as laboratories for experimenting with AI-driven narrative form and reader-player collaboration.6,9,12,18,20Creators can use platforms like AI Dungeon or custom LLM pipelines to prototype story ideas, test branching structures, and explore character interactions without the overhead of scripting every path, thereby augmenting human creativity rather than replacing it.18,20Likewise, hobbyist authors in the interactive fiction community may integrate AI as a co-author, using tools like Inform for structural logic and LLMs for drafting descriptions or dialogue.16,17,20However, Miller and others stress that technological novelty does not automatically translate into good storytelling; craft, ethics, and audience understanding remain essential.20AI tools must be guided by design principles that manage pacing, tension, and thematic coherence, and must be deployed in ways that respect user safety and diversity, given that LLMs reproduce biases from their training data.19,20As AI text adventures gain prominence, these considerations will shape both creative practice and policy around their use in entertainment and education.11,19,20## Future Directions for AI and Text Adventures

Integrating Symbolic Systems and LLMs

One promising direction for AI text adventures is hybrid architectures that combine symbolic world models with LLM-based narrative generation.15,19,20In such systems, a parser or state engine could manage the underlying game state—rooms, items, character attributes—while an LLM handles the linguistic realization of descriptions, dialogue, and narrative commentary.15,19This arrangement would restore some of the deterministic clarity of classic text adventures, ensuring consistent object behavior and puzzle logic, while still benefiting from AI’s ability to produce varied, expressive prose.2,4,15Symbolic components could also enforce rules about physics, magic systems, or social norms, preventing LLMs from granting impossible actions or ignoring consequences, thereby improving commonsense and internal consistency.19,20Multi-agent frameworks like STORYWRITER suggest that splitting responsibilities among specialized modules improves long-form coherence; extending this idea to interactive fiction could yield architectures where separate agents manage plot planning, state tracking, and localized scene writing.15,19Research in this area will have to address interface design, reasoning mechanisms, and user control, ensuring that players understand and trust the system’s behavior.15,19,20### Voice, Multimodality, and Immersive Experiences

Another trajectory involves extending text adventures into voice-driven and multimodal experiences, as seen in Wit’s End and the Minecraft murder mystery.10,12,14Voice input and output can make interactive narratives more accessible and social, allowing groups to play together without keyboard constraints and enabling more natural conversational styles.10,12,14Integration with AI-generated art, as in some platforms that create images based on scenes, can enhance immersion by providing visual cues and atmospheres without sacrificing the centrality of text.9,12,20As VR and AR technologies mature, AI-driven text adventures may evolve into mixed-media experiences where textual narration coexists with spatial environments, interactive objects, and sensory feedback, while LLMs manage dialogue and narrative adaptation.20The challenge will be to preserve the unique strengths of text—its capacity for introspection, ambiguity, and imaginative co-construction—within richer media contexts, ensuring that AI does not overwhelm or trivialize the reading experience.20Designers will need to balance interactivity, narrative depth, and usability, drawing on lessons from both classic interactive fiction and emerging AI narrative systems.16,17,20### Ethics, Safety, and Governance

Finally, future development of AI text adventures must confront ethical and safety issues, including content moderation, bias, and user wellbeing.18,19,20AI Dungeon’s history shows how explosive growth and limited moderation can lead to problematic content and reputational challenges, prompting ongoing debates about the governance of user-generated and AI-generated narratives.6,18Research on LLM-based GMing highlights the tendency of models to reproduce biases found in their training data and the absence of diversity in generated plots, underscoring the need for deliberate design interventions to promote inclusive storytelling.19Safety mechanisms such as filters, role conditioning, and human oversight, as implemented in the Minecraft murder mystery NPCs, demonstrate that it is possible to improve character consistency and prevent harmful outputs, but these measures require continuous tuning and evaluation.10,19Regulatory and institutional frameworks for digital storytelling and educational AI will likely play a role in setting standards for acceptable content, transparency, and user control, especially when text adventures are used in classrooms or therapeutic settings.11,20Navigating these issues will be integral to realizing AI’s creative promise in ways that respect players as co-authors and learners, rather than as passive recipients of algorithmic content.19,20## Conclusion

The relationship between AI and text adventure games encapsulates a broader transformation in digital storytelling, as deterministic parser-based worlds give way to fluid generative narratives that can adapt to nearly any input.2,6,9,20From the pioneering work of Adventure and Zork, which used MDL, ZIL, and the Z-machine to craft complex yet bounded interactive fiction, to modern platforms like AI Dungeon, Perchance, Infinite Worlds, and Wit’s End, the genre has expanded from carefully authored puzzles to open-ended improvisations across genres and modalities.2,3,4,5,6,9,12,13,14,18Large language models underpin this expansion, enabling responsive narrative generation but introducing new challenges of coherence, commonsense reasoning, and state tracking, which researchers are now tackling through multi-agent frameworks, hybrid symbolic–neural systems, and rigorous evaluation of GM-like capabilities.10,15,19,20Player experience in AI text adventures reflects these tensions: agency can feel both magnified, thanks to unconstrained input, and diminished, when the AI overrides character interiority or fails to maintain meaningful consequences; coherence can be compelling in short bursts yet fragile over long sessions.12,15,18,19Educational studies show that AI-generated interactive fiction can achieve narrative clarity and acceptable engagement, but that integration of learning tasks and maintenance of story-content coherence remain critical bottlenecks.11Creative practitioners and scholars emphasize that generative AI’s promise lies not simply in automation but in augmenting human storytelling, offering new tools for experimentation while preserving the craft and ethics of narrative design.20Looking ahead, the most fruitful path for AI and text adventure games likely lies in hybrid approaches that combine the structural rigor of classic interactive fiction with the expressive flexibility of LLMs, supported by careful world modeling, human oversight, and thoughtful integration of voice and multimodal elements.15,17,19,20As designers and researchers continue to explore this space, text adventures—once seen as a relic of early computing—may become a key arena for inventing new forms of collaborative, AI-assisted storytelling that engage players not only as readers and problem-solvers but as co-authors in evolving digital worlds.2,6,9,20
